# NOTES — the prime mover in Gaussian integers

Attached to the prime-mover page. It records the algebra behind the big fractions,
and the compression that keeps them readable.

## What a Gaussian integer is here

A **Gaussian integer** is a pair of whole numbers written `a + bi`. The `i` is a tag
that marks the second number as the vertical one. Nothing imaginary enters, and
nothing is rounded. `24 + 7i` is the point `(24, 7)`.

A **Gaussian rational** is the same pair divided by a whole number:
`(10296 + 11753i)/625` is the point `(10296/625, 11753/625)`.

## The step

The 7-24-25 triangle turns by `φ = atan(7/24) = 16.2602°`. In Gaussian form that
turn is multiplication by

    u = (24 + 7i)/25          length exactly 1, because 24² + 7² = 25²

Two triangles share each apex, so the apex advances by **twice** that angle —
multiplication by `u²`:

    u² = (24 + 7i)²/25² = (527 + 336i)/625

and `527² + 336² = 625²`, so the length is still exactly 1. This is "rotating the
first two triangles until they share the hypotenuse": the composition of two
reflections is a rotation by twice the angle, and in Gaussian integers that
composition is simply a multiplication.

## The whole run in one expression

Starting at `24 + 7i` and applying `u²` for `k` steps:

    apex(k) = (24 + 7i) · (u²)^k = (24 + 7i)^(2k+1) / 25^(2k)

and `25^(2k) = 625^k = 5^(4k)`. So **the entire sequence is one power**:

    apex(k) = (24 + 7i)^(2k+1) / 5^(4k)

Store `k`; regenerate the digits when needed. That is the compression.

## The fixed factors

Expanding `(24 + 7i)` in the Gaussian integers,

    24 + 7i = −i · (2 + i)³ · (1 − 2i)

so the real part always carries `2³ · 3 = 24` and the imaginary part always carries
`7`. Checked on all twelve stations:

    x = 24 · a      y = 7 · b      x² + y² = (25 · 625ᵏ)²

The hypotenuse is a pure power of 5, `5^(4k+2)`. So the numbers that matter are
`a` and `b`, and they are 24 and 7 times smaller than the raw coordinates.

## What this is, in geometry

The construction is an **orbit under a rotation group**, and saying it that way makes
the storage obvious.

- **A rotation about a point** is a congruence: it keeps every distance from the
  centre, so it carries the radius-25 circle onto itself.
- **Two reflections compose to a rotation** by twice the angle between their mirror
  lines. That is why a step of the walk is a rotation, and why it is the *square* of
  the triangle's own turn.
- The rotations `(24 + 7i)/25` and its square `(527 + 336i)/625` are elements of the
  group of rotations that keep the lattice rational — multiplication by a Gaussian
  rational of length 1.
- **The walk is the orbit** of the first apex under the subgroup generated by that
  rotation: `apex, R·apex, R²·apex, …`.
- **The figure is the orbit drawn**; the table is its coordinates; the chart is its
  projections.

**So the record is two things:** a generator of the rotation group and a seed point.
Everything else — every station, every digit — is derived. In the code that is

    WALK = { apex: (24, 7), rotation: (527, 336, 625) }

and `hitPoints()` applies the generator. Nothing else is stored.

## Rotating the first triangle: which rotations land on whole points

Take the first hypotenuse, the radius from `O` to `(24, 7)`, and rotate it about the
origin by an integer Gaussian rotation. Every rotation keeps the length at exactly
25 — that is what a rotation does — but only some land on a **whole-number** point:

| rotation | where the apex lands | whole? |
|---|---|---|
| `(24 − 7i)/25` | `(25, 0)` | **yes** |
| `(4 + 3i)/5` | `(15, 20)` | **yes** |
| `(7 + 24i)/25` | `(0, 25)` | **yes** |
| `(24 + 7i)/25` | `(527/25, 336/25)` | no |
| `(−7 + 24i)/25` | `(−336/25, 527/25)` | no |
| `(3 + 4i)/5` | `(44/5, 117/5)` | no |
| `(527 + 336i)/625` | `(10296/625, 11753/625)` | no |

**So the intuition is right, and it comes with a price.** Rotating by an integer
Gaussian rotation *can* create a new whole-number point on the same radius-25 circle
— `(15, 20)` and `(0, 25)` are two of the twelve. What it cannot do is create them at
*equal* angles: the integer points of `x² + y² = 25²` sit at uneven angles
(0°, 16.26°, 36.87°, 53.13°, 73.74°, 90°, …), because the circle carries only twelve
of them.

- **Equal steps** (32.52° each) keep the walk even and leave the integers after the
  first point.
- **Whole points** (twelve of them) keep the numbers whole and make the steps uneven.

You cannot have both on radius 25. That is the whole trade, in one sentence.

## Reading the compression honestly

- It **does** remove the noise: two numbers to look at instead of four, a factor of
  24 and a factor of 7 gone, and the whole station replaced by an exponent.
- It **does not** make the numbers small. `a` and `b` still grow, because the walk
  is exact and away from the twelve p-triple angles of radius 25.
- If small numerals are the aim, the only levers are: fewer steps, a smaller step
  (`(3,4,5)` divides by 25 rather than 625), a growing radius with whole numbers, or
  decimals that are labelled as samples.

## The spirograph, from the same generator

A spirograph is a sum of two rotations: one vane turning slowly, one small vane
turning fast, `P = a·Rᵏ + b·R^(m·k)`.

**The ratio must be a whole number**, and that decides the construction: both arms
have to come from the *same* generator `R = (24 + 7i)/25`. Two different p-triples
(16.26° and 73.74°) have an irrational ratio, so that pattern never repeats — the
first attempt at this drew a rosette with no closure, which is not a spirograph but a
quasi-periodic smear.

With one generator and an integer ratio `m`, the shapes are the classical ones:

| ratio | stations | shape |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 69 | two lobes, heart-like |
| **5** | 113 | **four-lobed rosette — the classic spirograph flower** |
| 7 | 157 | seven-lobed star |
| 9 | 201 | nine-lobed star, dense |

Every station is exact: each arm is `a·Rᵏ`, and `R` has length one, so the station is
a ratio of whole numbers. The drawing is a line between stations; the stations are
not sampled.

Code: `scripts/spirograph_walk.py`. Figure: `workbench/spirograph.svg`.

## The whole equation, and its simplification

Write `u = (24 + 7i)/25`, the step: length exactly 1, turn 16.26°. Then

    walker(k) = (24 + 7i)^(2k+1) / 5^(4k) = 25 · u^(2k+1)
    walker(m·k) = 25 · u^(2mk+1)

A spirograph is two vanes at amplitudes `α` and `β`, the second stepping `m` times as
fast. Every term written out:

    P(k) = α·walker(k) + β·walker(m·k)
         = 25α·u^(2k+1) + 25β·u^(2mk+1)

**Factor out the walker.** Both terms carry `u^(2k+1)`:

    P(k) = walker(k) · [ α + β·u^(2(m−1)k) ]

That is the whole curve: **the walker, times a bracket that is a single power of the
step.** Nothing of higher order survives, and the bracket is the only place `m`
appears.

**In whole numbers**, with `d = 5^(4mk)`:

    P(k) = (24+7i)^(2k+1) · [ α·25^(2(m−1)k) + β·(24+7i)^(2(m−1)k) ] / 5^(4mk)

The bracket is a Gaussian integer, the leading factor is a Gaussian integer, so every
station is **one Gaussian integer over `5^(4mk)`** — the coarsest grid the curve needs.

### What the simplification tells us

| case | bracket | curve |
|---|---|---|
| `m = 1` | `α + β`, a constant | `(α + β)·walker(k)` — a plain circle |
| `k = 0` | `α + β` | `(α + β)·(24 + 7i)` — one point |
| `m = 2` | `α + β·u^(2k)` | the simplest non-trivial spirograph |
| `β = 0` | `α` | the walker itself |

So a spirograph is **the walker modulated by a power of the step**, and the modulation
is the only thing that distinguishes one ratio from another. Verified numerically:
the factored form agrees with the direct sum at every station tested.
