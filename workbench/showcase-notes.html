Fig. 1a · exact

The 3-4-5 turn

A Pythagorean triangle gives a rotation you can write as fractions: the 3-4-5 gives cos = 4/5 and sin = 3/5, so turning a point is a multiplication with no trigonometry and no rounding error.

The turn is taken from the angle under 45°; the larger angle is its complement. That convention is why the ladder in Fig. 1b is built from 36.87° and not 53.13°.

  • cos 4/5 = 0.8
  • sin 3/5 = 0.6
  • angle 36.87°

Fig. 1b · exact

The integer Euler polygon and its rails

Eight equal chords, each turned by a whole number of triangle angles. Every node is an exact fraction, and a loop closes exactly when the turn numbers sum to zero.

The rails are a miter offset of the polygon. They are rational because every chord has the same length — one known number divides the perpendicular. This is the property that makes the polygon the exact object and the drawn curve the approximation.

  • 8 chords
  • turns sum to 8
  • rails rational

Fig. 1c · exact

Closure

The schedule 1,2,3,2,1,−1,−2,−3,−2,−1 sums to zero, so the chain ends heading exactly east with no angle drift. That is not a tolerance: it is an integer fact about the turn counts.

Niven's theorem explains why nothing else lands exactly on an axis — the tangent of a rational multiple of π is 0 or ±1, and a triple angle is never one of those.

  • schedule sum 0
  • exit heading east
  • drift 0

Fig. 3a · exact integers

Euclid family chain, n = 1

Take the fixed-n family of Euclid's formula, use each triangle's legs as a tangent vector, and step by the sum of consecutive tangents. Every node and every control point is an integer at scale 1.

C1 is exact at every join because both sides share the same tangent vector. Curvature is not continuous: the jump is 6.2% of the larger side (median) and 26.1% at worst.

  • integers at scale 1
  • C1 exact
  • 6.2% median, 26.1% max curvature jump

Fig. 3c · asymptotic

The exponent drifts to −4/3

A curve with κ ∝ sᵖ is a generalised Euler spiral: p = 1 is the clothoid, p = 0 a circle, p = −1 the logarithmic spiral. Fit p on the chain and it is not constant — it walks toward −4/3 as the band widens.

The algebra gives it: the turn is about 2n/m, the step grows like m², so the arc length grows like m³ and the curvature falls like s^(−4/3). n only scales the constant.

  • measured −0.835 (m ≤ 6)
  • −1.325 (m ≤ 400)
  • asymptote −1.333

Fig. 4a · exact

The exact taper

Two log spirals of the same pitch bound a band whose width is proportional to the radius. The partner is not merely scaled: it is rotated by a fixed angle, so the line between the two is perpendicular to the outer curve.

This is the only exact variable-width stem in the catalogue. It is also asymmetric: the outer half-width exceeds the inner by 5.25% at k = 1.12.

Fig. 4b · negative

A constant offset is not a log spiral

This is the most useful refusal on the page. Offset a log spiral by a constant distance and the result is not a log spiral: the angle between its tangent and its radius drifts, and the drift grows with d/r.

So "the log spiral has easy offsets" is true only in the sense of scaling — an offset whose width grows with the radius. A constant-width stem cannot be had this way.

  • drift 0.21° at d/r = 0.02
  • 2.59° at d/r = 0.20

Fig. 4c · negative

A parabola's parallel is not a parabola

The parallel family does exist exactly — as the involutes of the parabola's evolute, which is a semicubical parabola. Set the involute's arc-length shift to −1 and the original parabola comes back to 1e-15.

But those parallels are a different family. Fitting a parabola to the offset leaves a residual of about a fifth of d. There is no parallel parabola, and the construction proves it rather than denying it.

  • involute error 1.6e-15
  • best-fit residual 20.8% of d

Fig. 10b · exact, not a lattice

Logarithmic spiral

r = e^(0.22θ): the self-similar curve. Its exact property is that scaling it gives another spiral of the same pitch, so a band between two of them has a width proportional to the radius.

What it cannot do is hold a constant width — the offset drifts. And it has no lattice at all.

  • self-similar
  • width ∝ r
  • constant width drifts

Rays · composition

The rays, as a monstrance

The rays are the p-triple angles themselves: 2·atan(1/m) for m = 2…12, mirrored about the vertical axis. Their lengths fall with m so the fan closes, and the stem sits on the axis.

Every ray is the same object as Fig. 1a — one triangle's angle — used here as ornament rather than as a plan. That is the whole project in one figure: the same exact turn, repeated, becomes a leaf, a stem or a ray.

  • 24 rays
  • 53.13° at the centre
  • 9.5° at the tips
