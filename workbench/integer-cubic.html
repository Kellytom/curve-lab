Integer cubic Béziers

A Pythagorean triple gives a cubic Bézier with every control point an integer, a rational tangent, and a chord twice its hypotenuse. The 3-4-5 case is the base; every other triple is the same construction.

What was done, in plain English

We found an exact cubic family, not a fit. A Pythagorean triple supplies four integer control points; the 3-4-5 triple gives the base curve. The page then asks what that construction does and does not buy us.

  • It shows the family and ten triples, with every control point an integer.
  • It shows that Pythagorean rotations and a denominator scale keep the coordinates integer.
  • It records a 60³ sampling grid: 61 exact parameter samples after scaling, not integer points everywhere.
  • It separates the solid result from the open work: exact offsets do not follow, and a G2 join still has to be solved.
  • It records two alternatives for later: asymmetric integer arms and degree-elevated quadratics.

What is solid: integer control points, rational tangents, rational rotations at Pythagorean angles, and exact rational split points after scaling.

What is not solid yet: exact offsets, a working G2 join recipe, and a practical small-scale lattice. This is a construction page, not a finished production system.

The 3-4-5 base

(a,b,c)=(3,4,5) gives

P0=(0,0), P1=(3,−4), P2=(7,4), P3=(10,0)

chord 10, both arms 5, both tangents −53.13°. The control polygon is two congruent 3-4-5 triangles against the chord.

Rotations stay integer when the angle is Pythagorean

Rotate the 3-4-5 base by 53.13° (cos=3/5, sin=4/5) and scale by 5:

P0=(0,0), P1=(25,0), P2=(5,40), P3=(30,40)

Rotate by 36.87° (cos=4/5, sin=3/5) and scale by 5:

P0=(0,0), P1=(24,−7), P2=(16,37), P3=(40,30)

The angle between each arm and its chord remains 53.13°. An arbitrary rotation usually makes the coordinates irrational; a Pythagorean rotation makes them rational, and the denominator scale clears them.

What this means for the curve system

This is an exact integer-control spine, not a promise that every later operation stays integer. The useful question is which operations follow from the construction and which need their own test.

Points along the curve

Choose a rational parameter t=p/q. The de Casteljau construction gives rational control points with denominators dividing q³. Multiply the whole cubic by q³ and every control point, the split point, and both sub-curves become integer. For a finite set of fractions, use a common denominator Q and scale by Q³.

For the 3-4-5 base at t=1/3, scale by 27. The first sub-curve is

P0=(0,0), P1=(27,−36), P2=(57,−36), P3=(88,−24)

and the split point is (88,−24). The shape is unchanged; the lattice is simply finer. The parameter is a fraction of the curve’s parameter, not a distance along it, and the required scale can grow quickly.

Offsets are a separate promise

Integer controls do not make an offset curve integer. An offset point needs the unit normal, which involves a square root. The normal is rational at special points such as endpoints with Pythagorean tangents, but not at a generic point. The offset of a general cubic is not another cubic, and the 3-4-5 base is not a Pythagorean-hodograph cubic, so its exact offset is not rational in general.

Treat offsets as a fitted or numeric layer. Exact rails remain lines, arcs and PH curves; this family gives a clean spine to offset from, not an exact offset family.

G2 joins, symmetric and asymmetric

G2 needs both the tangent direction and the curvature to match. For an integer cubic, endpoint curvature is rational, so this becomes a rational algebra problem rather than a numerical fitting problem.

The symmetric family is a single shape parameter per triple: both arms have the same length, and the two endpoint curvatures have the same magnitude and opposite signs. That is elegant, but it is rigid at a join: matching one end fixes the curvature scale for the other.

An asymmetric version removes that constraint. Take

P0=(0,0), P1=(a0,−b0), P2=(L−a1,b1), P3=(L,0)

with integer chord L and two Pythagorean arms (a0,b0) and (a1,b1). The arm lengths can differ, and the two endpoint curvatures can be tuned independently. That is the natural way to give a piece a G2 match on both sides: rotate at a Pythagorean angle, translate on integers, choose or scale the arms for the two curvatures, then clear the common denominator.

This is a direction, not yet a proved recipe. A zero-curvature endpoint cannot be scaled into a nonzero one, the sign of curvature must match, and the required integer scale may be large. The asymmetric shape also moves the inflection, so it needs a shape and clearance check, not only a curvature check.

The mirror-join probe tests a narrower version: every symmetric triple can be mirrored at both ends and scaled by its hypotenuse so both joins are exactly G2.

The 60³ grid

A natural asymmetric example is the quarter-turn cubic

P0=(0,0), P1=(4,−3), P2=(7,−4), P3=(10,0)

It starts at slope −3/4, ends at slope 4/3, and has integer controls. The combined grid is 3³ · 4³ · 5³ = 60³ = 216000. The 2³ factor is already inside 4³; multiplying by it again would make the grid eight times finer than needed.

Every point at t=k/60 has a denominator dividing 60³, so scaling by 60³ = 216000 puts all 61 sampled points on one integer grid.

The 61 points are parameter samples, not arc-length points. The grid is a consequence of the integer controls and the chosen denominator; it does not make offsets or arbitrary curve points integer.

Another route: elevate a quadratic

A quadratic Bézier is a parabola. Degree elevation turns it into a cubic without changing the curve:

R0=Q0, R1=(Q0+2Q1)/3, R2=(2Q1+Q2)/3, R3=Q2

That is exact, not a fit. If the quadratic controls are integers, the elevated controls may acquire a factor of 3; scale by 3 to clear it. Points at a rational t=p/q have denominators dividing q² for the quadratic, which is cheaper than the cubic’s q³.

A single parabola cannot make an S: its curvature has one sign. An S needs two pieces, one on each side of the inflection. If you start with two quadratics and elevate them, the piecewise-parabolic S is exact for that design. If you start with the existing integer cubic S and split it at 1/2, each half is still a cubic; replacing each half by a quadratic is an approximation, not an exact elevation.

The trade is fewer shape controls. Two parabolas give a clean S and easy G1 joins, but G2 still requires a curvature match, and the family has fewer degrees of freedom than the asymmetric cubic. Parabola offsets are also not rational in general.

Worked example, and a correction

For y=x² on x∈[−3,3], the quadratic controls are (−3,9), (0,−9), (3,9). Elevation gives the cubic (−3,9), (−1,−3), (1,−3), (3,9). The handle vectors are (2,−12) and (−2,−12): slopes ±6 and length √148. The tangent intersection is Q=(0,−9), and each handle is two-thirds of the way from its anchor to Q.

A valid large integer parabola from the 3-4-5 family is P0=(−36000,24000), P1=(−12000,−8000), P2=(12000,−8000), P3=(36000,24000). Its Q is (0,−24000), its slopes are ±4/3, and its handle length is 40000. At t=1/8, 1/4, 1/2 it hits integer points (−27000,13500), (−18000,6000), (0,0).

A claimed “3/4” version with P0=(−48000,27000) has slopes ±9/8, not ±3/4, and its handle length is not an integer. It is still a valid integer-control parabola, but it is not the 3-4-5 example it claims to be. The 72000 scale is one chosen scale, not a universal grid.

Grid ledger: the canonical integer-cubic grid is 216,000 = 60³. The 72,000 figure is only the width of the large parabola's coordinate box, not a required scale. Use 360,000 = 72,000 × 5 only when you specifically need 1/25 samples on that large parabola; it is optional. Ninths add ×27; with 1/25 too, the common scale is 9,720,000. Do not take the 27 multiplier by default.

A simple 360-grid demo

The large example can be divided by 100 without losing the structure. On a [−360,360] grid, use

P0=(−360,240), P1=(−120,−80), P2=(120,−80), P3=(360,240)

The tangent intersection is Q=(0,−240); each handle is two-thirds of the way from its anchor to Q. The terminal slopes are ±4/3, the handle length is 400, and the control points are integers.

At t=1/8, 1/4, 1/2 the curve hits (−270,135), (−180,60), (0,0); the other quarter points mirror them. This is an exact elevated quadratic — one parabola — not the two-piece integer S. It is the simple demonstration version of the large-scale example.

The 3125 circle

The integer points on x²+y²=3125 have these slopes:

0.00°, 4.35°, 16.26°, 20.61°, 32.52°, 36.87°, 53.13°, 57.48°, 69.39°, 73.74°, 85.65°, 90.00°

Each is a rational-lattice rotation. Scale by 3125 to clear the denominator and return to integer control points.
