Catenary studies

Two projects running side by side: one hangs a catenary on pythagorean triangles, the other drapes a cloth on catenaries.

A · the catenary, chained from triples

A catenary asks for a tangent angle that changes with arc length: tan θ = s/a. The chain here may only take directions that a pythagorean triple can make, so each step snaps θ to the nearest rung of a 350-rung ladder, then lays a chord of the arc length that step deserves. Every chord is the hypotenuse of a real triangle, and every triangle is drawn: the legs are axis-aligned, so the chain is a staircase of exact triples you can hang other curves from.

The grid is in catenary units — fine every 10, heavy every 50, with a = 100. 20 chords: 10 down to the vertex and 10 up from it.

The two branches share one catenary. Down to the vertex is toward the focus; up from it is away. The split sets how much of the curve each side owns, and since height rises with arc, the side that owns more is also the higher anchor. At 0.62 : 0.38 the high anchor sits 71 above the vertex and the low one 32.

Fig. A · four hodograph cubics a side (ink) over the catenary (gold dashed) · arc 220, split 0.62 : 0.38 · worst error 0.0033% of a

Two dials, and nothing else. How many cubics a side, and where the low point sits. That is the whole control surface: each cubic is solved from two catenary points and their tangents, so there is no step length to choose and no rung to match. The error on each card is what the cubic cannot reach between its ends.

The same fit, snapped to a lattice. Solve the segments exactly, then round every control point to a whole unit. The pythagorean relation no longer holds — the legs stop being in geometric progression — so what comes out is an ordinary Bezier chain, and it leaves the catenary by 0.49% of a. That is the price of integer control points, and it is a price, not a rounding detail.

B · catenary drapery, in 2.5D

Weil's method is geometric, not physical: cloth is a grid of points, and the shape is found by fitting catenaries between the restraint points, splitting each triangle by the lower of the two candidate catenaries, then relaxing so the points keep their spacing. It aims at the look of cloth, not its properties. (The page it comes from is WPI's geometric techniques, which also covers Agui's sleeve folds.)

What is built here is the first half, the hanging: every row of the sheet is the catenary of its own rest width, so a row that has lost span sags more, and the cloth gathers toward the free edge. The relaxation pass is not in yet — the rows keep equal spacing down the sheet, which is why the folds read as neat rather than lived-in.

C · the hodograph chain

A cubic with a pythagorean hodograph is a p-triple drawn as a curve. With u(t), v(t) linear between two integer pairs, the control points are built from Euclid's formula, (u²−v², 2uv, u²+v²), and three things come free and exact: the arc length is a polynomial, the tangent angle is 2·atan(v/u), and the curvature is a rational function.

That gives the reverse gear. Given two points on the catenary and the tangent at each, a PH cubic through them is closed form: write Δ = p₃ − p₀ in the frame of the first tangent, let k = √(L₃/L₁), and the closure conditions give a quadratic in k. Pick the root with three positive legs; then u² + v² = 3L with arg(u + iv) = θ/2. No catalogue, no nearest neighbour, no search.

Two things called a triangle. In the catalogue-walk figures (Q–V) the gold triangles are the curve: each chord is the hypotenuse, and its corners lie on the curve because the curve is a chain of chords. In the hodograph figures the triangle is the control leg — scaffolding that hangs off the curve on purpose. At this scale the interior control points sit about 1.8 units away from the curve while the curve itself passes through every node to 10⁻¹⁴. So a card can look loose and still be exact; the dashed catenary underneath is the honest check, and in Fig. E you can see it dip below the ink at the vertex, which is the 0.41%.

Where the sines went. Nothing above needs them. A direction is carried as the integer pair (u, v); its triple is (u, v, h) with h = √(u²+v²). The half-turn the fit needs is then the angle between two integer vectors, which is a dot and a cross:

cos α = (u₀u₁ + v₀v₁)/(h₀h₁) · sin α = (u₀v₁ − v₀u₁)/(h₀h₁)

and the tangent's doubling disappears into Euclid, cos θ = (u²−v²)/(u²+v²), sin θ = 2uv/(u²+v²). Run both versions of the fit and they agree to the last digit — same segment, same control points, one with atan2, one with only dot and cross.

Fig. O · (i) the pair (4,3) doubles to the triple (7,24) — the tangent is twice the direction · (ii) one segment and its three legs, with L₁L₃ = L₂² · (iii) four segments over the catenary

What else a PH curve gives you. The offset — the curve moved sideways by a fixed distance — is rational, not an approximation: for z′ = w² the unit normal is −i·w²/|w|², so the plate at distance d is z(t) + d·(−2uv, u²−v²)/(u²+v²), evaluated exactly at any t. This is the Stage II plate without the re-fitting, and it is why PH curves are used for tool paths and road layouts.

Two of those are worth dwelling on. The split is exact: cut at any t and the halves are PH cubics with integer pairs again, so a curve can be refined without ever leaving the family. And the lattice condition is a number-theoretic test on the pair of directions — in a scan of 960 endpoint-and-direction cases, 158 had an exact rational square discriminant, so integer control polygons are ordinary, not rare.

Where a square root survives. One, and only one: k = √(L₃/L₁), the growth between legs. Solve the quadratic in k and it is exact whenever the discriminant is a perfect square — that is the arithmetic condition for a segment whose control points land on the lattice, and it is a property of the pair of triples, not of the curve.

Where the cubic and the catenary are the same curve. Position and tangent are exact at both ends by construction; the curvature is not. Measure it and the span decides everything: under about 20° of turn per segment the two curvatures agree to ~1% across the whole segment, so the cubic and the catenary are interchangeable there. Past that they cross twice and drift apart at the ends.

D · two more curves

The involute. The curve a taut string draws as it unwinds from a circle. Its speed is a·t — polynomial — and its tangent angle is the parameter t itself, so it is the most natural target the hodograph fit could ask for. Four cubics carry it to 0.0004 of a. The dashed spokes are the string: each is tangent to the circle and exactly as long as the arc it has unwound, which is the definition of the curve.

Fig. W · the involute (ink) over its own curve (gold dashed) · the base circle and four strings · three-leg triangles on every cubic

The bouncing ball, and why it is not a hodograph job. A parabola is exactly a quadratic Bézier — error 1.8×10⁻¹⁵ — so nothing needs approximating. Its control triangle reaches twice the apex height, drawn here in gold. And the joins are corners: the ball reverses at the floor, so there is no tangent to match and no G1 to keep. What is worth noticing is the heights: with elasticity e the apexes are H, e²H, e⁴H…, a geometric progression — the same shape as the doubling stacks, and the same corner machinery the spine layer already has.
