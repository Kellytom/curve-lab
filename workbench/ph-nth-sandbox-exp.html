The eleventh power and up

The septic sandbox stops at position degree 7, where the speed w is cubic. This page is the same machine one step up: r' = w^2 with w of degree 5, so twelve speed numbers and a position of degree 2 × 5 + 1 = 11. The offsets stay rational, the pitch is still the lcm of the integrated coefficients' denominators, and the closure algebra is unchanged — more terms, same rules.

A | the PH curve, from degree 11 up

Experimental. The rows below step by the exponent chosen at the top: 1/2 gives t^0.5, t^1.5, t^2.5 (5/2), t^3.5 (7/2). Bend and closest are measured from the curve. The grid and the inflection count need whole powers; when the step is 1/2 or 1/3 but every number still sits on a whole power — the half boxes left empty — the row is folded back and both are reported as usual. Only a number that really sits on a fractional power turns them off. A fractional step cannot be mirror-symmetric: symmetry needs t = 2^(-1/s), which is irrational for every rational step but 1.

One row of numbers for x and one for y, from t^0 upward. Each rung adds one number per axis, so the position degree rises by two: 11 up to 29, with 12 to 30 numbers. Doubling the rows is what lets a fractional step reach the same top power: at step 1/2, fifteen numbers per axis reach t^7. An even position degree cannot occur for polynomial speeds, so the readout never prints one. A curve saved at a lower rung loads here unchanged: the new boxes are zero, which leaves the shape alone. close chord drives the endpoint home; close to … holds a chosen openness instead, because a fully closed curve has no chord and bend is arc over chord.

The step is yours: filling the rows from a suggestion or from the dots never changes it.

Optical only: these change the frame, never the numbers. The knot-cutting controls that did not work are parked with their measurements.

The offsets are parallel here: r ± d n with d constant. Character offsets keeps the same construction but turns the speed a little at each station — g = w (1 + i ε b(t)) — and blends back to the parallel at both ends, so the rail leaves and arrives at exactly ± d and bows in between. The two character dials set how far each side bows (they can differ, which is what a serif needs), the profile chooses the bow's shape (one swell, an S that crosses the parallel, or a taper toward the end), end shear leans the rails along the tangent by the end, and tail hook adds h e^{iφ} t^5 to the speed itself, bending the last span without leaving the PH family. Note: how the flat spots build a curve

A flat spot is a t where the stroke runs dead horizontal. List the ones you want, press the button, and the boxes below fill themselves: the row is the polynomial those spots multiply out to. Mirror every spot about 1/2 — 1/9 with 8/9, 1/8 with 7/8 — and keep 1/2 for itself, and the curve comes out exactly symmetric. That is the only way to get symmetry on purpose; a random row almost never has it.

What to choose: the outer pair makes the knot (two swings out and back). Three copies of 1/2 make the long flat middle that lets the stroke cross itself; one copy is a gentle S, five copies is nearly a straight line and crosses nothing. Five spots is the cheap degree-11 rung; six or more moves you up a rung, which the page does for you. Bow leans the stroke sideways: 0 is straight, ±1 to 2 opens the loop, ±4 folds it hard.

Click low on the strip, beside the rule, to place a flat spot; click the same place again for another copy (the dots stack at the top of the strip), and click a dot to take that copy away.

Seven families: one outer flat spot, its mirror, three at 1/2

One flat spot and its mirror, plus three copies at 1/2, is five spots, so the row runs to t^5. The outer spot picks the family. Click one to load it, then nudge the numbers above to find its children; the dots it loads are on the strip in your own hand, so you can move one and watch the row follow.

Related families: two outer flat spots, its mirror, three at 1/2

Two outer spots on one side and their two mirrors, plus the three copies at 1/2, is seven spots, so the row runs to t^7. Each button carries the number of times that curve crosses itself, read from this page's own counter after loading it; the number in the button is that count. These seven are the best of the 171 such families over denominators 2 to 20: every pair was measured, and the list is by crossings first, then by the cheapest grid — 1/15 + 1/12 reaches nine at 1/182, where 1/11 + 1/5 costs 1/6,006 for the same nine. Adding a third outer pair reaches fifteen crossings at 1/3 + 1/7 + 1/11, but its grid is 1/19,399,380, so it is not offered here.

Possible families: what to investigate

Curves the census found that are not buttons above, chosen by hand for three different questions. record — the most crossings the box holds, at any grid cost, so the ceiling can be seen. cheap — five crossings or more on a grid of 100 or less, where the best value for the eye is likely to be. untried — one outer spot families outside the 1/15 to 1/9 window, so those seven are not mistaken for the whole set.

The count on each button is the census's, measured in exact whole-number arithmetic. This page evaluates in double precision, so on some of these rows it reads lower — four of the eleven read 0 — and a few draw as a flat line. Measured, not guessed: the rows that agree and the rows that do not are both on the list. If a button and the readout disagree, the census is right; the page is the limitation, and the loop window with a manual focus is the tool to try.

Rational rotation: a mirror you can turn

Turn the vertical mirror by an angle whose half-angle has a rational tangent, and the speed rows stay whole after a common factor. The axis turns with the curve: slope −3/4 at q = 1/2, −4/3 at 1/3, −15/8 at 1/4. At 45° the half-angle is 22.5°, whose tangent is √2 − 1: no rational rows, so the last panel is drawn from floats and says so.

How your numbers become the curve

You type two rows of numbers. The x row is how fast the curve moves sideways. The y row is how fast it moves up. Each number belongs to one power of t, and t runs from the start of the curve (0) to the end (1). The first number is the plain amount, the second is the amount that grows with t, the third with t squared, and so on. t^0 means t to the power zero, which is 1, so that first box is a constant that holds at every t; it is not a value at the start. If only the first boxes are filled the speed never changes, so the curve is a straight line: the later boxes are what bend it.

To get the curve, the speed is multiplied by itself, then added up from the start. Multiplying the speed by itself is the step that makes these curves special: the direction comes out of the product, and the length of the curve stays a whole number instead of a square root.

Multiplying a row by itself makes every number in the row meet every other number. Pairs that land on the same power are added together, and dividing by the new power turns a speed into a position. So the t term of the curve comes from just the two first numbers, the t squared term from the first three, and so on.

speed:        w(t) = p(t) + i q(t)      p is the x row, q is the y row
curve:        w(t) multiplied by itself, then added up from 0 to t

the t term of x     = x0*x0 - y0*y0
the t term of y     = 2 * x0 * y0
the next power      adds the next pair of numbers, divided by the new power

How high the powers reach is set only by how many numbers you type. Six numbers per row reach t to the fifth in the speed and t to the eleventh in the curve. Ten numbers per row reach t to the ninth and t to the nineteenth. That is why the ladder steps by two: the square of the speed always lands on an odd power.

First, the plain arithmetic

Powers add when you multiply, and signs follow a simple rule. That is all the grid below is doing.

Which numbers meet

Every number in the x row is multiplied by every other, and the cell shows the product. Cells on the same diagonal add up to one power of the curve: the corner cell alone makes the t term, the next diagonal makes the t squared term, and so on. The small number in each corner is that diagonal. The y row does the same sum, and it is subtracted.

Why the length has no square root

The length of an ordinary curve needs a square root, and the root rarely comes out clean. Here the two speeds are a matched pair, so the root cancels exactly and the length is a plain polynomial.

Which box does what

The low boxes set the start and the frame. The high boxes set the end. Changing the top x box by one moves the beginning of the curve by about a hundredth of a unit and the end by about a whole unit, so the top boxes are where you place the arm that has to meet the next curve. These numbers were measured on the default curve by changing one box by one and seeing how far the curve moved:

A high box also makes the arm longer: the last tenth of the curve measured 1.43 long by default, 2.05 with the top x box at 1, and 3.66 with it at 3. But a high box does not make the curve smaller by itself — it reshapes the end. It can shrink the whole curve only if you pull the end back with the opposite sign, which is a choice you make, not something the box does.

The start heading

The first pair sets the heading, and the heading is twice the angle of that pair. So a pair and its negative give the same heading — only the speed differs. The dark arrow is the curve on this page; it moves as you change the first boxes.
