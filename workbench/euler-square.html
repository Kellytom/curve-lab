A rounded square from eight Euler arcs

Tighten to 45°, relax to 90°, four times. Eight arcs and no straight runs: the corners meet each other.

Why eight. A corner has to leave a straight edge and arrive at another one, at right angles. One clothoid cannot do both ends: it has a single linear curvature law, so if it starts flat it cannot finish flat again. Two can — one tightening to the diagonal, one relaxing away from it, each the other's mirror image in curvature. Four corners, eight arcs.

What it buys. Curvature is continuous the whole way round: zero along each edge, peaking at the 45° diagonal, and no step anywhere. The circular-corner version on the generators page is one degree worse — a quarter circle meets its straight edge with an instant change of curvature, which is a visible kink on a cut path and an audible one on a machine.

Fig. 3 · the tight square, the squircle, and the family between

Pale tan is the eight-arc Euler square, blue is the cubic superellipse n = 3, gold is the squircle |x|⁴ + |y|⁴ = 1, and the dashed pale curves are n = 2, 6, 12. The Euler square sits outside the squircle of the same width, because its corners are built from a curvature tent rather than one formula: it is slightly fuller at the diagonal and quite a lot straighter along the sides.

At n = 3 the superellipse is 1.05% of size from the Euler square; the squircle is 3.63%. The cubic is the closest member of the family, which is why it is the one picked out in blue — the Euler square and the cubic almost coincide.
