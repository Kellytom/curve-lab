Generators

Integer planar PH curves of degree 3 to 6, from four numbers you can say out loud.

What this page is for, in plain words. A curve drawn on a screen is usually a chain of decimals. A curve drawn here is a chain of whole numbers, chosen before anything is drawn, so the same shape can be cut, measured or typed into another program without rounding. That is the whole point: pick four whole numbers, get a curve whose control points are whole numbers too.

The rest of the page is the arithmetic behind that, then a gallery of twenty named curves — the ones people actually draw — each with the grid it needs and the points where it lands exactly on the grid.

The input is the generator, not the curve. Every planar polynomial PH curve is written

z′(t) = h(t) · ( u(t)² − v(t)² , 2u(t)v(t) ),  σ = h · (u² + v²)

and Farouki and Sakkalis's lemma fixes its degree:

n = deg h + 2 · max(deg u, deg v) + 1

So the degree is chosen by the split, not by a multiplier. Cubics and quintics take h = 1; quartics take one linear factor; sextics take one linear factor and quadratic generators. Type integers into h, u, v, and a step 1/m, and the rest is arithmetic:

  • control points come from integrating a cubic (÷3), a quartic (÷4), a quintic (÷5) or a sextic (÷6), then converting to Bernstein;
  • milestones are the points at t = k/m;
  • the scale is the least integer that clears both, and it is read off the numbers you typed — not off the degree.

command: .venv/bin/python scripts/generators.py --n 5 --h 1 --u 2,1,3 --v 1,2,5 --m 5

Fig. 1 · where integer hits come from. A hit is a point of the curve sitting exactly on a lattice node. The natural first guess is that a finer grid finds more of them; it does not. Below is one curve — the bean — at two grids. At scale 12 it passes through 3 lattice nodes. At 48, and at 156, it still passes through 3: multiplying the grid finds nothing new, because the set of denominators that occur never changed. At 273 it finds 12, because 273 is built from the denominators that actually occur.

So the rule is the same one the scale table already gives, seen from the other side: the grid that collects hits is the one assembled out of the arithmetic, not rounded up to something round. And because a hit only needs its denominator to divide the grid, the grid is a factorisation problem. Searching the grids under 3125 for the best one per curve gives 801 hits across the twenty exact curves, against 112 at their control scales — seven times as many, with the same curves and no new mathematics. The table's last two columns carry that per curve: the count at the control scale, and the best count and grid under 3125.

A hit is counted over t = p/q with q ≤ 14: a stated sample, not a theoretical maximum. Every exact curve hits something — the floor is the crossings, which are integer by construction. What varies is how far the grid has to reach: the epicycloids at high k carry denominators in the hundreds, so under 3125 no grid beats 4, while the rounded square — built from integer pieces — reaches 456. The four sampled curves have no parameter at all, so they have no hits to count.

Two things fall out of that table, and neither is in the folklore. First, the milestone scale is mⁿ for a plain Bézier and never better for a PH curve: the PH property does not buy integer milestones, the step does. Second, PH costs you a factor of 3 (cubic), 15 (quintic) on the control points, because the integration denominator has to be cleared. What PH buys is elsewhere: exact arc length, rational offsets, a rational frame — not a smaller lattice.

Named curves: exact when rational, sampled when not. One cubic, four quartics and a sextic, and they split into two kinds. A curve is rational exactly when a rational parametrisation exists; then (X(t), Y(t), W(t)) turns into an exact Bézier with whole numbers throughout, by substituting t = s/(1−s). Where no parametrisation exists there is nothing to turn, and the curve can only be sampled.

Why the bean has no polygon. Its homogeneous control values include two with W = 0 — points at infinity. The integral data lives in three dimensions and the picture is its shadow, so there is no closed polygon to lay on the lattice.

But integer milestones do exist. The bean's quarter-points are (1, 0), (90/91, 30/91), (2/3, 2/3), (10/91, 30/91), (0, 0): denominators 1, 91, 3, 91, 1, so a scale of 273 puts every one on integers. Any finite set of rational parameters clears, because you take the lowest common multiple — the scale is a fact about the arithmetic, not a wall.

What is a wall. No bounded curve holds infinitely many lattice points, so the chat log's "infinitely many points on the curve can be integers" cannot be true of any curve, PH or not. You get finitely many, at parameters you choose, and the denominators set the price.

The two lemniscates are both rational. Gerono's x⁴ − x² + y² = 0 is a figure eight from one Bézier. Bernoulli's (x²+y²)² = x² − y² looks like it should not be — its arc length needs elliptic integrals, which usually means genus one — but its shape is genus zero, because the double point at the origin and the two circular points at infinity account for all of the degree. The parametrisation x = (t+t³)/(1+t⁴), y = (t−t³)/(1+t⁴) is exact, and one lobe is one Bézier. Rationality is about the shape, not about how hard the perimeter is to measure.

Hypocycloids are the same family with one sign flipped. Inside a fixed circle of radius R = kr the rolling circle traces x = m cos θ + cos(mθ), y = m sin θ − sin(mθ) with m = k − 1, and k counts the cusps directly. k = 2 is the Tusi couple — a straight line, which is why the degenerate case is worth naming rather than skipping; k = 3 is the deltoid; k = 4 is the astroid, already above. Two more are shown at k = 3 and k = 5. The astroid above and the hypocycloid at k = 4 are the same shape in different normalisations — one is cos³t, the other 4cos³t — which is the kind of thing that looks like an error and is not.

Epicycloids are a family, and two members are already here. A circle of radius r rolling outside one of radius R = kr traces x = r(k+1)cos θ − r cos((k+1)θ), y = r(k+1)sin θ − r sin((k+1)θ). At k = 1 that is the cardioid and at k = 2 the nephroid — both on this page already, in their own normalisations. The degree climbs with k: 2(k+1). Two more are shown, k = 3 at degree 8 and k = 5 at degree 12, and they stay rational throughout, because cos(mθ) is a polynomial in cos θ — Chebyshev again — so tan(θ/2) still rationalises the whole thing.

The nephroid is the sextic that is a cubic in disguise. z ↦ z³ + 3z sends the unit circle to it, which is why the parametrisation is so short: x = 6cos φ − 4cos³φ, y = 4sin³φ, both polynomial in cos and sin, hence rational in tan(φ/2). Two cusps, genus zero, exact.

The astroid is a sextic with four cusps. x^(2/3) + y^(2/3) = 1 looks like it needs fractional powers everywhere; in the form (x²+y²−1)³ + 27x²y² = 0 it is a plain sextic, and x = cos³t, y = sin³t becomes X = (1−u²)³, Y = 8u³, W = (1+u²)³ under u = tan(t/2). Four cusps, genus zero, exact.

Roses are the same trick at higher degree. The trifolium (x²+y²)² − x³ + 3xy² = 0 is a quartic with three petals, and y = tx through the triple point gives x = (1−3t²)/(1+t²)². The quadrifolium (x²+y²)³ = (x²−y²)² is a sextic — four petals need the extra degree — but still genus zero, so it too has an exact Bézier, of degree six.

The squircle's integer-compatible cousins. Within the superellipse family |x|ⁿ + |y|ⁿ = 1 the rational members are exactly n = 1 (a diamond), n = 2 (a circle) and n = 4/3 — which, written as x^(2/3) + y^(2/3) = 1, is the astroid. n = 4 is genus 3 and cannot be rational. So a convex square-cornered shape has to be assembled: the rounded square below is four straight edges and four quarter circles, and each quarter circle is exact under the tangent-half-angle substitution, with no √2 weights — x = c + (1−s²)/(1+s²), y = c + 2s/(1+s²). Eight pieces, all integer data, one shape.

The squircle is the honest failure here. |x|⁴ + |y|⁴ = 1 is smooth: no node, no cusp, nothing for a line through the origin to exploit. Its genus is 3, so it has no rational parametrisation at all, and it is sampled. That is the line the whole table is drawing — singularities make curves cheap, and the squircle has none.

The cardioid is the same machinery again. r = 1 − cos φ is a circle rolling round a fixed one; in Cartesian it is (x²+y²)² + 2x(x²+y²) − y² = 0, a quartic with a cusp at the origin. With u = tan(φ/2) it is X = 2u²(1−u²), Y = 4u³, W = (1+u²)²: four integers, four powers, one Bézier.

Two genus-zero quartics that differ only by a bound. The Gerono lemniscate x⁴ − x² + y² = 0 and the cruciform x²y² − b²x² − a²y² = 0 are both rational quartics, so both have exact rational Béziers. The lemniscate is bounded — it closes into a figure eight — so one Bézier over t = s/(1−s) covers all of it. The cruciform runs off to infinity along both axes, so no single Bézier covers it: each of the four arms is a separate window, and the page draws a finite piece of each. Same genus, same degree, different answer — the bound is what decides.

The bow is the triple-point case. x⁴ = x²y − y³ crosses itself three times at the origin, which is what makes it genus zero and rational: every line through the origin meets the curve once more, so x = t − t³, y = t² − t⁴ is the whole curve. It is polynomial, so its Bézier is exact with no weights at all — the cheapest named curve on this page.

Two nodal cubics, and only one is the PH one. Every cubic PH curve is a scaled, rotated Tschirnhausen cubic, 27ay² = (a−x)(8a+x)², whose node crosses at 60°. The curve above it, y² = x²(x+1), has a 90° node — so it is a nodal cubic but not that one, and it is not a PH curve at all. The node angle is the tell, and it is easy to measure: both curves are the same genus and the same degree, and an affine map can turn one into the other, but no similarity can, because an angle is not affine.

The control cases fail, and that is the point. The pear x² = y³(1−y) is a quartic with a cusp at the origin — genus 2. The heart (x²+y²−1)³ = x²y³ is a sextic with three cusps, at (±1, 0) and (0, −1) — genus 4. Both are real, both are drawable, and neither has a rational parametrisation: |∇F| vanishes at those cusps, which is exactly what rules it out. They are drawn by solving for y at sampled x. A table where every named curve came out rational would prove nothing.

The piriform is the fourth exact one. a⁴y² = b²x³(2a − x), which at a = b = 1 is y² = x³(2 − x): a rational quartic with a cusp at the origin, a vertex at (2, 0), and the bounds 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, |y| ≤ 3√3/4. Through x = 1 + sin t, y = cos t (1 + sin t), then u = tan(t/2), it becomes X = (u+1)²(1+u²), Y = (u+1)²(1−u²), W = (1+u²)² — whole numbers, no clearing needed.

A caution recorded here because it cost an hour: x⁴ − x²y + y² = 0 is a quadratic in y with discriminant −3x⁴, so its only real point is the origin, and x⁴ + x²y − y² = 0 factorises as (y − k₁x²)(y − k₂x²) — two parabolas, not a pear. Only the x³ form is the curve.

Where the 3,125 grid comes from. It is not a property of quintics. It is 5⁵: the step 1/5 raised to the degree 5, the same rule as mⁿ above. For the curves on this page the table gives it exactly — this quintic stepped by fifths: 93750 · by tenths: 1500000 · this cubic by eighths: 192.

So the mesh number is fixed by the degree and the step, and by nothing else; the generators only decide whether the control points clear cheaply. And a scale is only a minimum: any multiple works, so the power-of-two grids (128, 256, 2048) are reached by rounding the computed scale up, with the margins free.
Where the paste is right: you can move factors into the generators. Type u = (2,8), v = (4,16) and the scale falls, because common factors cancel inside the curve before the mesh ever sees them. Where it is wrong: nothing moves mⁿ, because that term comes from the Bernstein weights at a rational t, not from the curve.

What this does not cover, and why.

  • n = 2. There are no quadratic PH curves: the lemma gives n = 1 or n ≥ 3.
  • The bean, the piriform and the heart. They are quartic algebraic curves, not PH: their speed is not a polynomial, so their offsets are not rational. They still take integer control points — every Bézier does — but their milestones cost m⁴ and nothing about them is exact. Draw them as plain quartics on the same lattice; do not expect the PH machinery.
  • The catenary. A true one needs cosh, so it is transcendental and no scale factor can put it on an integer grid — the AHPH family keeps the PH property in the hyperbolic basis, but not integers. The catenary page already does the integer-friendly thing: four Tschirnhausen cubics over the curve, worst error 0.41% of a.
  • The 2^n grids. 128, 256, 2048 are mesh sizes, not mathematics: any scale that is a multiple of the computed one works. Pick the power of two above the required grid and the margins are free.
