Levien integer sandbox

Simple conditions first: a parabola y = x², a half parabola y = x²/2, a quarter parabola, a sixty-fourth parabola y = x²/64, and one integer cubic.

What you are seeing. Ink is the source, blue/red are the exact normal rails, gold-dash is Levien's fitted cubic rail, and the grid is the integer lattice. The dots are the known integer points on the source. The note counts near-integer points on each rail (tolerance 0.01) and the Levien fit error.

The simple result. The source parabola has integer points (0,0), (1,1), (2,4); the half has fewer; the quarter fewer still. A parabola's exact offset is a higher-degree rational curve, but not a cubic; for a general cubic the exact offset is degree 10. The vertex at t=0 gives an integer point when d is an integer; at d=2 both exact rails and the Levien fit hit the vertex (0,±2). Elsewhere the normal contains √(1+4t²), so an exact integer point is a Diophantine question, not an automatic one; scaling can clear denominators. Levien's rail is a real-valued fit, so it is not expected to land on integers either. Use the selects to look for the exceptions; the controls in the generator are the variables to reverse-engineer.

Levien’s logic

Why fit at all. “The exact offset curve of a cubic Bézier can be described (it is an analytic curve of degree 10) but it [is] not tractable to work with.” So the practical method is an approximation.

The method is curve fitting. “The fundamental concept is curve fitting, or finding the parameters for a cubic Bézier that most closely approximate the desired curve.” Levien then uses numerical support: cusp finding, area/moment fitting, quartic root finding, and an error measure.

One cubic may not be enough. “A single cubic Bézier might not be a good enough approximation to the parallel curve of the source cubic Bézier, so in those cases it is [subdivided] into multiple Bézier segments.”

The error is the judge. Candidates are thrown into the mix and “let the error measurement sort it out.” The error is measured by sampling the exact curve and projecting each sample onto the approximation along the normal — the Tiller–Hanson test — so subdivision is driven by measured distance, not by a fixed count.

Cusps are where the fold is. “These happen when the curvature of the source curve is equal to one over the offset distance.” Levien finds and subdivides at the cusps before fitting, because a cubic cannot carry the reversal.

Scaling. “If a curve is divided in half, the error of the approximation will decrease by a factor of 64” — the claimed O(n⁶) scaling. The earlier Euler-spiral approach was O(n⁴) (a factor of 16), which is why the cubic fit is attractive.

What it means for the integers. None of this constrains the fitted control points to integers. Levien chooses the cubic that best matches the exact parallel, so the rail is real-valued. That is why this sandbox searches families and d with a tolerance, rather than assuming an integer hit; exact integer routes remain lines/arcs, PH and the Pythagorean spiral. The p-triple Euler polygon is the other integer-friendly route.

Quotes are short excerpts from Raph Levien, “Parallel curves of cubic Béziers” (2022-09-09), local copy: reference/external/parallel-beziers.html. See the article for the full derivation and code.

The mechanism, in formulas

1. The exact target. Write the source cubic as B(t), its derivative as B′(t), and the unit left normal as N(t). The exact parallel at signed distance d is:

B(t) = (1−t)³P₀ + 3(1−t)²tP₁ + 3(1−t)t²P₂ + t³P₃
B′(t) = 3(1−t)²(P₁−P₀) + 6(1−t)t(P₂−P₁) + 3t²(P₃−P₂)
N(t) = (−B′y(t), B′x(t)) / |B′(t)|
Od(t) = B(t) + d N(t)

For a general cubic this is an analytic curve of degree 10. It is not a cubic Bézier, so the fit replaces it with one.

2. Fix the free parameters. The endpoints and the tangent directions are taken directly from the exact offset. That leaves only the two handle lengths λ₀ and λ₁:

Q₀ = B(0) + dN(0), Q₃ = B(1) + dN(1)
T₀ = B′(0)/|B′(0)|, T₁ = B′(1)/|B′(1)|
C₀ = Q₀, C₁ = Q₀ + λ₀T₀, C₂ = Q₃ − λ₁T₁, C₃ = Q₃

The handles lie on the two endpoint tangents. They are not constrained to a parabola; the two scalars are what the area/moment equations determine.

3. Measure area and moment. The target is reduced to two numbers. Green’s theorem turns the double integrals into line integrals; the code evaluates them with 32-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature:

A* = ½∫₀¹ y(t) x′(t) dt, M* = ½∫₀¹ x(t) y(t) x′(t) dt
∫₀¹ f(t) dt ≈ ½ Σ wi f((1+ξi)/2)

After rotating the chord to the x-axis and translating its start to the origin, normalize by the chord length L: A = A*/L² and M = M*/L³. The fit is then scale-free.

4. Solve for the handles. With the chord normalized to (0,0)–(1,0), let θ₀ and θ₁ be the tangent angles. The signed area of the candidate cubic is:

A(λ₀,λ₁) = (3/20)[2λ₀ sinθ₀ − λ₀λ₁ sin(θ₀−θ₁) − 2λ₁ sinθ₁]

Setting A(λ₀,λ₁) = A gives λ₁ linearly in λ₀:

λ₁ = (2λ₀ sinθ₀ − 20A/3) / (λ₀ sin(θ₀−θ₁) + 2 sinθ₁)

Substitute that into M(λ₀,λ₁) = M. Clearing the denominator gives a quartic in λ₀:

a₄λ₀⁴ + a₃λ₀³ + a₂λ₀² + a₁λ₀ + a₀ = 0

The coefficients are polynomials in A, M, sinθ₀, sinθ₁, cosθ₀, cosθ₁; the explicit expansion is in Levien’s cubic_fit (reference lines 1452–1480). Each non-negative root is a candidate. A near-zero or negative length is clamped to the tangent intersection and still sent to the error test.

5. Pick the best candidate. Each candidate is transformed back to the original frame, then sampled against the exact offset. For each sample, the code intersects the normal ray with the candidate cubic and takes the maximum normal distance (Tiller–Hanson). If that error is above tolerance, the interval is split at t = ½ and the process repeats.

6. The parabola. For y = x² on x ∈ [0,2], the source quadratic has P₁ = (1,0); degree elevation gives the cubic handles:

Q(t) = (1−t)²(0,0) + 2(1−t)t(1,0) + t²(2,4)
P₀ = (0,0), P₁ = (2/3,0), P₂ = (4/3,4/3), P₃ = (2,4)
Od(t) = (t − 2dt/√(1+4t²), t² + d/√(1+4t²))

The quadratic handle (1,0) is the intersection of the end tangents. It is not on the parabola; neither are the elevated cubic handles. The exact offset is a higher-degree rational curve: set u = √(1+4t²) − 2t, then

t = (1−u²)/(4u), √(1+4t²) = (1+u²)/(2u)
x(u) = t(1 − 2d/√(1+4t²)), y(u) = t² + d/√(1+4t²)

So a parabola’s parallel is rational, but it is not a cubic. A general cubic’s exact parallel is degree 10. Levien’s two handles are still cubic handles chosen by area and moment; there is no parabolic handle constraint.

The handles on a parabola. The useful 64 example is y = x²/64 on x ∈ [0,24]: the cubic handles are (8,0) and (16,3), and the integer points are (0,0), (8,1), (16,4), (24,9). More generally, on [0,24n] the handles are (8n,0) and (16n,3n²), all integer. Note that the lattice is sparser in x — x must be a multiple of 8 — not denser; the gain is integer handles, not more points per unit x.

For y = kx² over x ∈ [a,b], the quadratic control is the tangent intersection, and degree elevation gives:

P₀ = (a, ka²), P₁ = ((a+b)/2, kab), P₂ = (b, kb²)
P₁′ = ((2a+b)/3, k a(a+2b)/3)
P₂′ = ((a+2b)/3, k b(2a+b)/3)

For a = 0 and b = 3n, these become (n,0) and (2n, 3kn²): integer handles for integer k. That is a real integer-friendly source. A cubic is a parabola exactly when C₃ − 3C₂ + 3C₁ − C₀ = 0; Levien does not impose that condition, so his fitted cubic is general, not a parabola.

Why 3ⁿ, 5ⁿ and 64 appear. Evaluate a cubic Bézier at a rational parameter t = p/q:

B(p/q) = [(q−p)³P₀ + 3(q−p)²pP₁ + 3(q−p)p²P₂ + p³P₃] / q³

With integer controls the denominator divides q³. So t = 1/3 gives 27, t = 1/4 gives 64, t = 1/5 gives 125; scaling by q³ clears the denominators. This is a Bézier-basis fact, and it is a different “64” from Levien’s error law: the error 64 is 2⁶, while the sample denominator 64 is 4³. Both can produce the same-looking integer hits, but only denominator clearing is exact.

7. Scaling: 64, 3⁶, 5⁶. The claimed error law is E(m) ≈ E(1)/m⁶. Halving gives 2⁶ = 64; three pieces give 3⁶ = 729; five pieces give 5⁶ = 15,625:

This is approximation error, not a count of integer hits. More segments mean more control handles, so there are more chances to notice a near-integer coordinate; it does not make the curve exact. Scaling the whole geometry by S multiplies every coordinate by S: a rational point whose denominator divides S can become integral (the q³ rule above), but an irrational fitted coordinate remains irrational. The error factor 3⁶ is 729; the sample denominator 3³ is 27; do not conflate the two.

8. Hyperbolae and other sources. A hyperbola H(t) = (a cosh t, b sinh t) has tangent (a sinh t, b cosh t) and normal

N(t) = (−b cosh t, a sinh t) / √(b²cosh²t + a²sinh²t)

For a rectangular hyperbola the square root is √(cosh 2t), which is not generally rational in a rational parameter. The offset is therefore not an automatic rational/integer curve. Levien’s fit still applies — area, moment and normal distance can all be evaluated numerically — but exact integer hits are a Diophantine property of the source, not a consequence of the fitting method. The exact integer-friendly families remain lines, arcs, PH curves, the Pythagorean spiral, and the p-triple polygon.

Where the bounding-parabola idea lives

Blog, cusp finding (2022-09-09). The sentence is in “Parallel curves of cubic Béziers,” section “Cusp finding”: raphlinus.github.io/curves/2022/09/09/parallel-beziers.html#cusp-finding. It is a prose aside; the post links no cusp-specific bounding-parabola code.

Full design, cubic/cubic intersection (2022-08-16). raphlinus/raphlinus.github.io#79 “Path operations” is the detailed write-up. It proposes x = ay² + by + c, fits it by matching endpoints and signed area (or moments), bounds the cubic with two hybrid quadratics, and claims Δy³ scaling — “convergence should be cubic.” It also estimates the stop criterion as ε−1/3, roughly 100 segments at ε = 10−6.

Prototype (2022-08 / 2023-05). linebender/kurbo#258 is the draft cubic/cubic intersection branch. The interesting file is examples/cubic_fun.rs; see EstParab, from_cubic, and intersect_cubics_rec. It is not merged and not wired into kurbo.

Follow-ups. kurbo#277 says “My approach is to compute bounding parabolas. A prototype exists in #258”; kurbo#274 separates the intersection-result trait from the bounding-parabola primitive. jneem/linesweeper is a later Rust boolean-ops implementation based on #258; it is archived and has moved to Radicle.
