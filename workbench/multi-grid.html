curve-lab · composition

One design, three grids

Nothing forces a design onto a single ruling. Each part can declare the grid its own detail needs, as long as every join is a point that exists in both grids — which is automatic when the grids are a chain of multiples.

Fig. G1 · stalk, connector, flower

The stalk is on integers because it only has to land on lattice marks. The connector needs fifths to meet the flower's forty-five degree seams. The flower is on twenty-fifths. The dashed lines are declared seams, not accidents.

The rule

A part on grid 1/d can join a part on grid 1/e exactly when the shared point's coordinates are multiples of 1/lcm(d,e) — true whenever the finer grid is a multiple of the coarser. The composite grid is the lcm, and the cost is the number of cells it needs:

partgridcells per unit
stalk1/11
connector1/55
flower1/2525
composite1/2525

Three parts, 25 cells — cheaper than putting every part on the finest grid from the start, and exactly as exact.

Fig. G2 · the per-span version, and its price

Give each span of a turn-exact chain its own grid — 5, 25, 125 … — and the design is legal; but any single global grid that holds all of them is the product, 5^k. Six spans cost 15,625 cells per unit, twelve cost 244 million. That is the same explosion as the scaling table, now visible as a layout cost rather than a number-theory one.

The practical rule: per-part grids are cheap when the parts are few and the multiples are small; per-span grids are cheap only for short chains. Past about six spans, the composite grid is no longer a drawing surface but a data structure.
