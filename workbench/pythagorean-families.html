Pythagorean triples, rays and parabolas

This is the field of triples, not a hand-picked list. Multiples of a primitive triple lie on rays from the origin. For a fixed Euclid n, varying m gives a parabola a=(b/(2n))²−n²; the n=2 family passes through the 43.6° triple (20,21,29) and descends to the 4° region.

A parabola catalogue is infinite: each parameter value is a curve. The useful catalogue is by family and intercept law. The fixed-n family is the green fan; fixed m is the gold fan; Fässler's cluster is the rust band.

In a 500×500 box: the fixed-Euclid-n family has n=1..15 because 2n²≤500; the fixed-Euclid-m family has m=1..22 because m²≤500. The roughly 22 downward curves are the gold fixed-m family; the right-opening green fixed-n family is the other set.

Fässler's parabolas: a second slicing

There is a useful equivalent parameterization. Put N=(b+c)/2 with the even leg as a. If a²/(4N) is an integer, then

(a, |N − a²/(4N)|, N + a²/(4N))

is a Pythagorean triple, and every triple can be written this way after possibly exchanging the legs. The triples therefore lie on

b = |N − a²/(4N)|

a parabola reflected in the a-axis. This N is not the same as Euclid's n above: the fixed-Euclid-n curves and the fixed-N curves are two different slicings of the same triple field. The plot above uses the Euclid slicing; both are valid.

At a=2N the parabola meets the axis with derivative −1, so the reflection is at 45°. Swapping a and b gives the perpendicular family, and the axes therefore carry the crossing pattern described in the source.

Holding Euclid m instead of n gives another family; with a=2mn, b=m²−a²/(4m²). That is the other set of smooth parabolic slices visible in the scatter.

Near N≈1450 several values are close together: 38²=1444, 2·27²=1458, 3·22²=1452, 5·17²=1445, and 10·12²=1440. Their parabolas nearly coincide, which is why a dense parabolic strip appears in the scatter.

Source: Wikipedia, “Pythagorean triple”, distribution section; the references there point to Albert Fässler and conformal-mapping treatments.

Fig. C1 · five triples, four integer spans

The assembly is a triple tangent chain: each triple is used as a tangent, not as a chord. Span k runs from node Nₖ to Nₖ₊₁ with control points

P₀ = Nₖ   P₁ = Nₖ + Tₖ   P₂ = Nₖ₊₁ − Tₖ₊₁   P₃ = Nₖ₊₁

and the node step is Dₖ = 2·(Tₖ + Tₖ₊₁), so every control point in the figure is a whole number. At a join the incoming derivative is 3·Tₖ and the outgoing derivative is 3·Tₖ — the same vector, not just the same direction. The chain is therefore C1 exactly, by construction rather than by fitting.

The step factor is 2, not 1. At Dₖ = Tₖ + Tₖ₊₁ the inner handles satisfy P₂ = P₁, every span degenerates to a quadratic and its end curvature is exactly zero — a measured zero that means nothing. The factor 2 separates the handles and leaves the joins a genuine curvature comparison.

Fig. C2 · the whole sweep

The same construction run over all 53 arm vectors: 52 spans, 53 nodes, integer controls throughout, C1 at all 51 joins. The first four spans are the dark segment at the left; at this scale the individual turns are small, which is the point — the sweep is a gentle 40° bend, not a set of separate curves.

Curvature: the joins are C1 but not G2. Measured in exact rational arithmetic, the curvature jump is 6.2% of the larger side at the median join and 26.1% at the worst. That is near-G2: no tangent breaks, and no join carries a visible corner.

What the sweep actually is

The angle is not a free choice and it is not logarithmic. In the fixed-n family a=m²−n², b=2mn, so the arm angle is θ(m)=atan(2nm/(m²−n²)), which falls off like 2n/m. For n=2 that is about 229/m degrees: the last point has θ·m = 229.1, converging on 4 rad·(180/π) = 229.2.

A power fit over the sweep gives θ ∝ m^-0.988 (R²=0.9999). So the progression is algebraic — the fixed-n family is a parabola in the (a,b) plane — not exponential and not logarithmic.

Along the chain, curvature follows an even cleaner law: |κ| ∝ s^-1.238 with R²=0.9928, where s is arc length. That is a spiral, but not a familiar one: a clothoid is κ ∝ s, a logarithmic spiral κ ∝ 1/s, a circle κ ∝ s⁰, and this is -1.24. The sweep bends sharply at the start and almost straightens by the end, which is exactly what the figure shows.

G2 is available — but not on a grid

Fixing one handle length for the whole chain leaves curvature jumps. Letting each node have its own handle hₖ does not disturb C1, because both sides of a join still share hₖ — and it gives one equation per join. Writing Cₖ = Tₖ × Tₖ₊₁, the join curvature difference of the two spans simplifies to

Cₖ (S − hₖ) = Cₖ₊₁ (S − hₖ₊₂)

— a two-step linear recurrence, rational throughout. With h₀=h₁=1 the handles come out 1, 1, 29/23, 37/30, …, all positive, and the curvature difference at all 51 joins is exactly zero: the chain is G2, not nearly G2.

The catch is integrality. Those fractions have a common denominator of 84 digits, so clearing it produces a grid of absurd size. C1 is free on the integers; exact G2 costs the integers. The honest summary: this chain is integer C1 and rational G2, and the two do not meet here.

Measured by pyth_chain.g2_report() and pinned by tests/test_pyth_chain.py: the zero gap, the recurrence's first fractions, and the size of the denominator are all asserted.

Reading it as a curve sequence

The plot exposes candidate chains: choose a family/parabola, move along it, and use each triple as an arm vector or angle. The n=2 sequence is the first candidate for a G2/G1 chain; it is a list of exact integer arm pairs, not a fitted approximation.

This page maps the triples; the next probe is to turn one family sequence into a chain and compare its G2/G1 joins. It does not claim the whole field is one curve.
