What G3 means, and why the circle is the easy case

These notes sit beside the cubic G3 showcase and Quintic Béziers every join G3. They explain the continuity ladder in ordinary language and record the curves that pass G3 trivially, or fail it when they are built as independent spans.

The ladder, at magnification

G0: the spans meet. G1: magnify the join and the direction matches — there is no corner. G2: the bend matches — there is no kick in the steering. G3: the rate at which the bend changes matches — the steering wheel accelerates smoothly, so the change of bend has no step either.

G3 is the first order that reads as smooth acceleration rather than merely smooth shape. It is invisible in a small drawing and visible in a large one: magnify the join and a G2 curve still shows a small change in how the bend is changing, while a G3 curve does not.

The circle is G3 everywhere

A circle has curvature κ = 1/R at every point and curvature-rate dκ/ds = 0 at every point. The bend is constant, so the acceleration of the bend is zero on both sides of any join. The circle therefore passes the G3 gate at every point without any construction. That is why it is not a showcase panel: it would be a true but trivial pass. The showcase uses curves whose G3 joins are not free.

Independent spans versus a collapsed cubic

G3 does not require the spans to be cuts of one polynomial. Two independent Béziers can be joined G3 if their control points are chosen so that the position, tangent, curvature and curvature-rate match. The showcase does exactly that.

There is still a special case worth naming: if the underlying curve is a cubic, the spans can collapse to cubics. Chopping a cubic into cubic pieces does not create a new curve at each knot, so all derivatives agree. Figs. 3 and 5 use that case. The bell, the log spiral and the sine wave are not cubics, so their independent spans are quintic instead.

The bell is not a cubic, so it cannot collapse to one. Eight independent four-point spans, solved for G3 at every join, carry it with a worst Δ(dκ/ds) of 6e-13 and a peak error of 7.5e-6. The four-cubic attempt is the rejected case; eight spans are what it takes. The control points are real, not integer.

Why independent cubics usually fail

A cubic Bézier has four control points. A generic G3 join fixes position, tangent, curvature and curvature-rate at both ends — more conditions than four points can carry. The four-cubic bell was measured and rejected: its curvature-rate defect at the inflection is about 1.9. Degree-7 spans carry the same data without that defect; the gate on the showcase page is the evidence.

The finished curve, not the span

G3-complete is the claim that matters for a finished drawing: a finite partition into arcs such that every interior join — the closure seam of a closed form and every mirror seam included — matches position, tangent, curvature and curvature-rate, and every break is declared by the source (a cusp, a node, a singular endpoint), not created by the fit. A smooth handover adds one path with no declared feature and a deviation under 5% of the curve size.

Mirror. A curve crossing a mirror axis orthogonally is automatically G3 at the seam: reflection preserves κ, so κ is continuous; κ is even about the seam, so dκ/ds is odd and must vanish there. A true mirror of a smooth half therefore passes, and dκ/ds = 0 is the test that the mirror is true. An oblique crossing fails G1; a mirror tangent (opposite tangents) is a cusp, declared as one.

Closure seam. The start point of a closed curve is an artifact of the parameterisation, so the seam must pass like any other join. If it fails, re-knot, or put the seam at a declared node or cusp. A nodal loop is complete with that node, not smooth.

Native degree. A quintic span is a pure degree elevation of a cubic when the fifth divided difference is zero: P0 − 5P1 + 10P2 − 10P3 + 5P4 − P5 = 0. The badge cubic-native means the fifth-degree freedom did no work — a selector signal derived from the control polygon, never asserted.

The gate

At each join the page measures |Δp|, the tangent angle, |Δκ|, and |Δ(dκ/ds)|. The pass thresholds are 1e-9 and 1e-6°. The gate is computed from Bézier derivatives, not from a sampled polyline: the sampled drawing is for the eye, the table is the claim.
