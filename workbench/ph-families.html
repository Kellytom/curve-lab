curve-lab · PH families

PH families · degree ladder, integer hits, Hermite and piecewise

2D only. Every planar PH curve is z'(t) = h(t)·(u²−v², 2uv) with n = deg h + 2·max(deg u, deg v) + 1. With h=1 only odd degrees appear; the h factor is the only route to an even degree. Model coordinates, +y up — the flip to screen y happens in the SVG frame at output. Blue lines are the axes; every figure shows all four quadrants.

Definitions, in one place

PH · polynomial Pythagorean-hodograph curve

A planar curve whose speed is a polynomial: x'² + y'² = σ². Then the arc length is a polynomial, the tangent frame is rational, and the offset is rational instead of refitted. If the speed is only rational the curve is rational PH — the circle is the example.

PH–Hermite · the PH quintic Hermite interpolant

The PH quintic through two points with prescribed endpoint tangent directions: z(0)=P₀, z(1)=P₁, arg z'(0)=θ₀, arg z'(1)=θ₁. With w = a(1−t)² + 2bt(1−t) + ct² and z' = w², the endpoint condition is one quadratic in b: 2b² + 3(a+c)b + (3a² + ac + 3c² − 15Δ) = 0. The speeds |a|² and |c|² are free, so this is a two-parameter family per endpoint pair — not one curve. This page's declared rule is |a| = |c| = √|Δ|.

Not called PHH here. In the CAGD literature PHH means a PHH-Bézier curve — a PH curve built to approximate a hyperbolic H-Bézier curve (see the box below). The Hermite interpolant is not that, so this page spells it out as PH–Hermite and keeps PPH for piecewise PH, which is what the literature means by it too.

PPH · Piecewise Pythagorean-Hodograph

PH spans joined with C² continuity: at each join z, z' and z'' agree. With z' = w² and z'' = 2ww', that means the next span's a is the previous span's c, and its b is fixed by the previous end slope. The join is exact by construction, with no fitting. A general C² spline solve — five conditions per join, both ends free — is not built.

The spatial/helix reading of the interpolant and PPH is out of scope: the operator's only self-imposed limitation is to stay in the plane.

What the literature calls these — and what this page calls them

The names collide, so here are the sources in their own words before this page uses them.

“In Computer-Aided Geometric Design (CAGD) PPH curves most commonly refer to Piecewise Pythagorean-Hodograph curves (or occasionally Planar Pythagorean-Hodograph curves). A Pythagorean-Hodograph (PH) curve is a special type of polynomial parametric curve whose derivative (its hodograph) satisfies a Pythagorean condition. Connecting multiple PH segments together forms a Piecewise PH (PPH) curve, which extends these exact geometric properties to complex, long-span paths and splines.”

“For a standard 2D parametric curve r(t) = (x(t), y(t)), calculating its speed or arc length requires taking the square root of the sum of squared derivatives √(x'² + y'²). A PH curve avoids this by requiring x'(t)² + y'(t)² = σ(t)² — the square root cancels, so the speed ‖r'(t)‖ = σ(t) is a pure polynomial.”

“Single polynomial PH curves have fewer degrees of freedom than ordinary Bézier curves of the same degree. Designers therefore join lower-degree PH segments (most commonly PH cubics or PH quintics) into a Piecewise PH spline: cubic PPH splines can achieve C¹ or C² continuity, and quintic PPH curves allow positions, tangent directions and curvatures to be matched (G² or C² Hermite interpolation) at every waypoint. Arc length is then a polynomial integral, and parallel offsets r_d(t) = r(t) + d·n(t) stay exact rational curves.”

“There is considerable interest in the properties of PH curves in geometric modeling and CAGD because PH curves can be computed at speeds comparable to polynomial curves and used to calculate curve arc lengths and isometric lines. The purpose of this paper is to develop a general approximation of the H-Bézier curve based on PH curves. We call the resulting approximations PHH-Bézier curves for convenience. … It is demonstrated that cubic PHH-Bézier curves can accurately approximate H-Bézier curves but that the selection of the middle two control points of the PHH-Bézier curve has a profound impact on the quality of the approximation.”

In plain language. PH is a curve whose speed is a polynomial, which makes arc length exact and offsets rational. PPH is several PH pieces joined end to end so their tangents — and, with quintics, their curvatures — agree at the joins; that is how you follow a long path without a monstrous single polynomial. PHH, in the literature, is something else again: a PH curve chosen to imitate a hyperbolic Bézier curve (the H-Bézier family, used for catenaries and hyperbolas), with the middle control points blended to keep the error small. It is an approximation technique, not an interpolation problem.

So this page says: PH for the family; PH–Hermite for the quintic through two points with prescribed tangent directions (the two free speeds above); and PPH for piecewise PH, as the literature does. It does not use the acronym PHH at all, because the literature has already spent it on the H-Bézier approximation, and a second meaning would be a trap.

The construction, degree by degree. Write the hodograph as z' = w² and expand w in the Bernstein basis. The endpoint condition ∫₀¹ w² = Δ is then one polynomial equation in the middle coefficient.

  • Cubic (w = a(1−t) + bt, four real parameters; similarity uses two, so two shape parameters): (a² + ab + b²)/3 = Δ, hence b = (−a ± √(3(4Δ − a²)))/2. The control points follow by integrating w²: P₀, P₀ + a²/3, P₀ + (a² + ab)/3, P₀ + Δ. Four integer parameters in, integer control points out — which is the triangle construction on the integer-cubic page.
  • Quintic (w = a(1−t)² + 2bt(1−t) + ct², six real parameters; four shape parameters, two after the endpoints and tangent directions are fixed): 2b² + 3(a+c)b + (3a² + ac + 3c² − 15Δ) = 0, solved on this page and in curve_lab/ph_hermite.py. With b chosen, the endpoint is hit exactly.

Both formulas are checked numerically: the cubic one to 1.8e-15 over 500 random real and complex (a, Δ), the quintic one by tests/test_ph_hermite.py.

The degree ladder

n = deg h + 2·max(deg u, deg v) + 1. h=1, u,v linear → 3 · h linear, u,v linear → 4 · h=1, u,v quadratic → 5 · h quadratic, u,v linear → 5 · h linear, u,v quadratic → 6 · h=1, u,v cubic → 7 · h linear, u,v cubic → 8. A rational h gives rational PH (the circle). n = 2 exists only rationally.

The trap. pyth_cubic is exact but not PH: its name is the control triple, not the hodograph. The cubic PH family is the Tschirnhausen alpha — one shape up to similarity.

n = 3 · the full nodal Tschirnhausen alpha PH

The whole alpha, not a segment. The node sits at t = ±√3 and the two endings run to the right. Up to similarity this is every cubic PH curve. The heavy gold arc is the C-segment t ∈ [−1, 1] that `hodograph.py` draws.

n = 3 · the alpha at ×3 — integer controls PH

The same alpha with h = 3: the control points become (0,0), (0,2), (2,4), (6,4), all integers, and ten lattice points fall in the same window — (0,0), (6,±4), the node (18,0), (24,±4), (54,±36), (96,±104). Every integer point is (6k², ±2k(3−k²)) plus the node; the family is complete by the same argument as the alpha's. Scale changes the count; the shape cannot.

n = 3 · a second cubic (same shape) PH

A different generator pair, a scaled and rotated copy of the same Tschirnhausen curve: the cubic rung has one shape, not a family.

n = 4 · PH quartic PH

The even degree needs deg h = 1. It inflects, which the cubic cannot.

n = 4 · a second quartic PH

Another h, another shape: the quartic rung is a family, not one curve.

n = 5 · PH quintic, u, v quadratic PH

The workhorse: C and S shapes, one-segment Hermite interpolation, the lowest degree that carries a C² spline.

n = 5 · PH quintic, h quadratic PH

A quintic reached the other way: 2 + 2·1 + 1 = 5. Same degree, different generator split, different shape.

n = 5 · PH quintic, u = 1+t², v = 2t PH

A symmetric member: u − v = (1−t)² and u + v = (1+t)², so both ends meet the same side.

n = 6 · PH sextic PH

Even degree from h linear with quadratic generators: the multiple-cusp shapes live here.

n = 6 · a second sextic PH

A second h and generator pair at n = 6; one card is not the family.

n = 7 · PH septic PH

h = 1 with cubic generators: 0 + 6 + 1 = 7.

n = 7 · a second septic PH

A second cubic generator pair at n = 7: the odd rung is a family too.

n = 8 · PH octic PH

The n = 8 rung: deg h = 1 with cubic generators, 1 + 6 + 1 = 8. The general n = 8+ ladder is not enumerated; this is one member.

n = 8 · a second octic PH

A second h and generator pair at n = 8; the ladder stops being a single example as soon as one more h is chosen.

n = 3 · integer cubic 3-4-5 not PH

An exact integer-control cubic, not a PH curve. The control triple is Pythagorean; the hodograph is not, so the 3-4-5 base has no rational offset. See integer cubics.

rational rung · the circle PH

There is no polynomial PH quadratic. The rational circle is the n = 2 rung: the speed is rational, so the arc length and the offset are rational — but not polynomial.

The scale grows as mⁿ times the denominators of the control points. PH never improves that: a plain integer Bézier pays the same mⁿ and already has integer controls at scale 1. What PH buys is the polynomial arc length, the rational frame and the rational offset.

Integer points on the curve itself — the real numbers

Two different things carry the word “hit”, and they are now drawn differently. A milestone is integral after the scale above is applied: it exists by construction, and its coordinates are large. An integer point of the curve is integral in model coordinates, with no scale at all. Filled red circles are the second kind, labelled with their coordinates; hollow blue squares are the first, drawn at t = k/m and unlabelled because their value is coordinate × scale. The interactive explainer

Fig. 20 · the live figure; its caption is drawn inside it and names the rung and scale.

The sliders move only PH-valid generators: any polynomials u, v, h give a PH curve, so no slider setting can leave the family. The grid m recomputes the exact hit count and scale in the browser; the quadrant boxes mirror; the offset slider shows the exact PH offset beside a fitted normal offset; and “break PH” shifts a control point, after which the drawn speed is no longer the polynomial σ and the identity fails — the readout says by how much.

The blue labels are exact, not rounded. A blue square sits at a fractional point of the drawing — at m = 4 the alpha's t = 3/4 point is (9/8, 39/32) — and the label gives the integer it becomes after multiplying by the scale: (108, 117). That multiplication is done in BigInt rationals, and a test checks the browser's scale against generators.PHCurve.scale, so the pair on the label is the exact integer, not an approximation of it. Zoom (slider, wheel, or click) separates points that overlap at full view.

This is the solution, in one box

  1. Pick a grid on the parameter, not on the page. m is a count: you agree to look at t = k/m for k = 0 … m. That is all m means — how finely the curve's own parameter is divided. A bigger m means more points and a bigger cost, never a different curve.
  2. Each of those points is a fraction. On the alpha, t = 3/4 gives (9/8, 39/32) — not integers, and no rounding will make them exact.
  3. Multiply the whole curve by one number. Take the least common multiple S of every denominator actually present — the four control points and the m + 1 chosen points. Multiplying by S clears them all at once, and only then are the coordinates whole numbers. For the alpha on t ∈ [−4, 4] at m = 4, S = 3 and the five points become (96,104) (24,4) (0,0) (24,-4) (96,-104). On t ∈ [0, 1] at the same m the quarter points cost S = 96 instead — the scale is a fact about which points you chose, not about the curve.
  4. That is the grid they fit. The integers are measured in units of 1/S of the model: S points per unit. Nothing was moved or fitted; the curve was never changed, only the unit it is written in.
  5. The bound is the degree. S is at most mⁿ times the control denominators — n is the degree — and cancellation can make it much smaller, as it does above. The bound is what makes a whole m = 12 grid on an octic cost tens of millions before any cancellation. More m buys more points and risks more digits.

The interactive below applies exactly this rule, and the grid points are now drawn across the whole range of the curve, not just t ∈ [0, 1]: every blue square is labelled with the integer it becomes at the scale the readout names.

PH–Hermite and PPH, pinned

The exact Hermite data. Two points and two tangent directions; the tangent magnitudes are the free parameters, and the shape moves with them. The endpoint is hit exactly because the quadratic in b is solved for it.

The join. endpoint miss 0.0e+00 · |Δz| 0.0e+00 · |Δz′| 0.0e+00 · |Δz″| 0.0e+00 · |Δκ| 0.0e+00. The blue span is not fitted: its a is the black span's c and its b is set by the black span's end slope, so the join is C² by construction.

A general C² PPH — five conditions per join, both end tangents free — is not built. What is proved here is the one-sided rule and one two-span chain.

Interpolation — from the curve to cubic spans for Inkscape

The blue-sky question: use an interpolated Euler/clothoid chain as the middleman, and hand Inkscape a few cubic Béziers that start and end on integer lattice points. For the Tschirnhausen the middleman is not needed: the alpha is a cubic, so splitting it is exact. A split is not a fit — the spans are the curve, not an approximation of it.

Fig. 26 · the alpha cut at t = −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3 into six cubic spans, one path. Deviation 0 and worst Δ(dκ/ds) 0 are not roundings: de Casteljau on one cubic reproduces it exactly, and the joins are the same curve. The full curve needs no interpolation at all.

A six-span quintic G3 interpolation of the same curve measures 0.148 units — 0.49% of its size — with worst Δ(dκ/ds) 3.9e-15. That is what the interpolation buys when the curve is not cubic; here it is not needed.

The p-triple Euler polygon is the other middleman, and it makes many exact rational nodes — 121 of them at 120 chords, 7 at 6. Their common integer scale is the catch: on this section the 6-chord polygon needs a 4,507-digit scale, and more chords do not help (the digits are dominated by the triple's hypotenuse, not by the chord count). That is why the page quotes 5⁶ = 15,625 for six 3-4-5 spans and nothing larger: the polygon gives exact nodes, but integer nodes cost an exponential scale. For six spans or fewer it is the best lattice route there is; for a whole curve it is not.

Two fitted rails at ±0.60. Levien's cubic fit, not an exact offset: the alpha's offset is rational, but a cubic-Bézier rail is a fit, and the error is printed on the figure.

One branch section, node split: the branch t ∈ [0, 1.5] stops short of the node at t = ±√3, so the rail never has to cross itself. Rails at ±0.22.

Integer curves from the triangles — nothing to fit

A cubic Bézier is four points: two anchors where the span starts and ends, and two handles that pull the curve without lying on it — the two round dots you drag in Inkscape. Every curve below is built from a Pythagorean triple, and all four of those points are whole numbers (gold: the control polygon and its handles; red: the anchors). That is the difference between a curve that happens to pass through lattice points and a curve whose handles are the lattice: open it in Inkscape, and both handles sit on the grid, so dragging one cannot take it off.

The m = 10 milestones — the same fourteen rungs, marked

Figures 1–14 above are the rungs as they are. Below are the same fourteen curves, unchanged, with the m = 10 milestones marked on each: the points you get by cutting the curve's own parameter into ten equal steps. Eleven per curve — the two ends and nine between them — so 154 milestones across the fourteen. (The PH-INTEGER notes call these milestones; a lattice station is a different thing — a point where one coordinate is an integer — so this page says milestone.)

In plain words, what setting m = 10 does. The curve is not touched. You take its parameter — the dial that runs along the curve from its start to its end — and turn it to eleven fixed positions: 0, 1/10, 2/10 … 10/10. Those eleven positions are eleven real points on the curve, and in ordinary coordinates most of them are fractions. Then you multiply the whole curve by one number, the ×S printed on each figure, and every one of the eleven becomes a whole number at the same time. So the eleven hollow squares are eleven exact lattice points, all of them, once the unit is 1/S. Nothing is fitted and nothing is rounded — the scale is chosen, not searched for.

Why ten? It is a readable density, not a special number. Raise it and you get more milestones and a larger `S`; lower it and you get fewer. The limit is the digits in `S`, not the geometry. Red circles are the curve's own integer points at scale 1; hollow blue squares are the m = 10 milestones, integral at the printed scale.
