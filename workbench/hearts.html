Hearts

One Euler arc, rotated until it kisses the y axis, then mirrored. The cleft is the kiss; the little extra is where it carries on past it.

The expanded set — fifteen formulaic hearts — is a separate page; this page is the clothoid-heart proof.

What the mirror needs, and what is still open. For the join to be smooth the arc has to meet the y axis at two places tangentially — it runs along the axis at both, so the mirror continues without a kink. After a rotation φ the vertical-tangent points are fixed by s²/2 + φ = π/2 + kπ; the two contacts lie on one line only when their rotated x agree. For one half mirrored about the axis, both contacts must be on that half; on a finite unit arc the gap never reaches zero — those contacts crowd toward the clothoid's eye and only tend to it (table below). Fixing the arc's endpoints, as the figures below do, leaves only the rotation free and one contact — which is why the bottom of Fig. 1 is a corner rather than a blend. The full ± curve is a different pair: rotated 38.5° clockwise, its two outer turns are level to under 0.0001, a usable table contact (clothoid-cut Fig. 5). The mirror-axis join still needs two placed arcs or a stated gap.

The rotations below are therefore not a family to pick from: they are the rotation at which the far end is tangent, for each half turn the arc completes. Any of them draws a heart. The first is merely the smallest.

Every percentage on this page, and on every page in the workbench, is an error: a distance between what was drawn and what was wanted, as a share of a stated reference. Where a number is not an error it says so in words.

Double tangency: the half mirror join

This table is the same-half mirror join: both contacts on one half. For each turn count the arc was rotated to the closest approach; the two vertical-tangent contacts never share an x. They crowd toward the clothoid's eye, so the gap shrinks with every doubling of turns and reaches zero only in the limit. The full ± curve's table contact is a different pair — under 0.0001 at 38.5° CW (clothoid-cut Fig. 5).

Closest always at φ = 90°. The closest pairs sit near the eye, so they are not heart-sized lobes: this is a limit, not a join. The full ± outer-turn contact is the usable join; the same-half axis join needs two placed arcs or a stated gap.

The heart, split at x = 0

The heart is symmetric in x, so it is two curves. Taking the positive-x half alone gives one open arc from the bottom point to the cleft — the shape we actually have to build. Correct method is a clothoid chain fitted to that arc, not a chord walk: the blue dashed line drawn over the curve is an Euler polygon against the same samples. The sample path now closes on the lobe tip, where the two y-roots merge, so the polygon is judged on the geometry rather than on the old jump.
