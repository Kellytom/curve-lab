Euclid family chains · integer editor

An Euclid family chain is built from arm vectors, not points: take the triangles of one fixed-n Euclid family, turn each into a tangent Tₖ=(a,−b), and step S·(Tₖ+Tₖ₊₁). The handles are Tₖ, so both sides of a join use the same vector and C1 is exact whatever you choose. The two knobs that matter are which triangles you take and how long the handles are.

Each triangle is normalised so that its b leg is the x step while its height keeps that triangle's own a/b shape; triangle k starts exactly where triangle k−1 ends, so the apex chain is a rising stem. The band starts at m=2 — the 3-4-5 itself, 53.13° — which is the tight end. A width is only legal while d < R_min, the smallest radius of curvature anywhere on the chain, and that radius sits at the seed, so it sets the width for the whole stem. The rails are the true offset at ±d; the gap between them is measured, not assumed. Verdict: see the line under the figure.

Fig. X · the two routes, side by side

Where the names come from. Euclid's formula turns two whole numbers into a right triangle: a = m² − n², b = 2mn, c = m² + n². Fix n and let m run, and you get a family of them — for n = 1: 3-4-5, 8-6-10, 15-8-17. Euclid families is therefore the set of triangles (the field plot on pythagorean-families.html); Euclid family chains is the curve built from one of those families, using each triangle as a direction. Turn-exact is the other route (pythagorean-spiral/). Neither name claims a role: both routes can be a stem, a spine or a chain. They differ in what stays exact — turn-exact keeps the turns and the points exact and fits the ink; Euclid family chains keep the ink exact (integer control points at scale 1) and fit the turns.

Fig. X1a · turn-exact: the triangle is an angle

The m triangle of the n = 1 family drawn as an angle (above 45° take the complement; the ladder itself holds angles under 45°): turning by it is multiplying by (a/c, b/c). Every turn divides by c, so the point stays exact as a fraction.

Fig. X1b · Euclid family: the triangle is a vector

The same triangle drawn as a vector: the tangent is (a, −b). Every step adds whole numbers, so the point stays an integer.

Fig. Y · the exponent drifts to −4/3

A curve with κ ∝ sp is a generalised Euler spiral: p = 1 is the clothoid, p = 0 a circle, p = −1 the logarithmic spiral. Fit p on the n=1 chain over a band m = 2 … M and it is not constant, but walks steadily toward −4/3: for large m the turn is θ ≈ 2n/m (so dθ/dm ≈ −2n/m²) and the arc length grows like m³, which puts the curvature at s−4/3. The exponent does not depend on n; n only scales the constant. So the chain is not a new species — it is a member of the family, with a finite-band exponent that drifts.

What the page is asserting

C1 is exact in every configuration, including the uniform-handle one: the derivative at a join is 3h·Tₖ from both sides. G2 is not free — with a single handle length the curvature jumps; the per-node handles button solves Cₖ(S−hₖ)=Cₖ₊₁(S−hₖ₊₂), which drives the measured gap to zero but makes the handles rational, so the control points stop being integers.

Integer C1, rational G2 — the same trade the page pythagorean-families.html records in Fig. C1/C2. Model: curve_lab/pyth_chain.py. This page recomputes everything live in JavaScript; if the two ever disagree, the page is wrong.
