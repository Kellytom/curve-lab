Euler match

Every exact named curve, replaced by a chain of equal chords whose heading is the curve's own tangent. Gold is the chain, ink is the curve, red is a node.

What is being measured. Deviation is an error: how far the chain strays from the curve, as a percentage of the curve's own size — the same currency as the fidelity budget. Snap is how far each node has to move to sit on that curve's integer grid; it is reported in grid units, because a snap of 0.3 units on a grid of 273 is a third of a pixel while the same 0.3 on a grid of 5 is enormous.

The chord count is the smallest that lands under 1% of size. Halving the chord halves the deviation — that is the whole dial. Three chains are compared, because they answer different questions:

  • Euler polygon — equal chords, heading taken from the curve at each node. First order in the chord, and the cheapest thing that works. Under 1% on every curve here.
  • One clothoid — a single quadratic heading over the whole curve. It is the honest test of "is this curve clothoid-shaped", and it fails on almost all of them: 35% on the bean, 45% on the nodal cubic, 72% on the Bernoulli lemniscate. A single linear-curvature law cannot follow a curve whose curvature changes sign.
  • A chain of clothoid spans — one quadratic heading per span, continuous in heading, curvature jumping at the joins. This is what the spine layer does, and it works: 0.44 to 1.1% on bean, nodal, Tschirnhausen, trifolium, quadrifolium, Gerono, piriform, Bernoulli, circle and the rounded square, at 4 to 32 spans.

What it cannot do is a cusp. The astroid, deltoid, cardioid, nephroid and the epicycloids all carry real cusps, where the tangent reverses in no distance at all. A clothoid turns finitely, so the chain cuts the corner and the deviation stays between 9% and 63% however many spans you spend. Those curves want a corner in the chain — a joint, not a blend — which is the `Segment.corner` the engine already has.
