curve-lab · Pell and the integer hyperbola

x² − 2y² = 1: the lattice points that approximate √2

One integer point on this hyperbola is enough to generate every other. The doubling law is the same algebra as the Pythagorean triple doubling, with the Gaussian integers replaced by Z[√2]; the ratios are the convergents of √2, and the error is exactly 1/(2√2 y²).

Fig. R1 · the hyperbola, its lattice points, and the asymptote

(3,2) and (17,12) are the first two points after the unit (1,0); every one of them satisfies x² − 2y² = 1 exactly, so x/y is a rational approximation to √2. The dashed line is y = x/√2 — irrational, so the integer points approach it but never touch it.

Fig. R2 · the error law, measured

For any point, (x/y)² − 2 = 1/y², so |x/y − √2| = 1/(y²(x/y + √2)). The product error × y² therefore tends to 1/(2√2) = 0.353553. The plot is that product, and it is already at the limit by the fourth point.

The two laws

Both are one line of integer arithmetic. Doubling: (x,y) → (x² + 2y², 2xy). Adding: (x₁,y₁)·(x₂,y₂) = (x₁x₂ + 2y₁y₂, x₁y₂ + x₂y₁). The second is multiplication of x + y√2, so the norm x² − 2y² multiplies: (x²−2y²)(u²−2v²) = (xu+2yv)² − 2(xv+yu)². Start at norm 1 and every product has norm 1.

Doubling gives 3/2 → 17/12 → 577/408; adding (3,2) gives 3/2 → 17/12 → 99/70. Both are Pell sequences; the addition law and the doubling law agree because 17/12 is already (3,2)².

Why it belongs beside the p-triple plane

The Pythagorean triples come from units in the Gaussian integers: (u+iv)² doubles an angle and produces the next triple. Pell comes from units in Z[√2]: (x+y√2)² doubles the “hyperbolic angle” and produces the next point. The integer cubic family uses the first; this page is the second, on the same lattice, with the same exactness rule — integers and Fractions, never a floating point claim. The difference is the conic: a circle has finite order, the hyperbola has infinite order, which is why Pell has infinitely many integer points and the circle only finitely many.
