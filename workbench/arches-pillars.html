curve-lab · arches and pillars

One section, mirrored: the join decides

Take a section from the showcase, place it, mirror it about the centreline. If the two ends meet inward at the crown it is an arch; if they leave the stem and branch outward it is a capital. The curve does not change between the two rows — only where it is placed and which way it faces. Each panel names its section and measures the join.

Arches · the halves meet inward

The crown is the test: a well-set arch arrives near vertical, so the two mirrored tangents meet with little kink. Measured off-vertical: integer cubic, half 16.2°, cycloid, half arch 3.9°, astroid, quadrant 8.5°. These are rigid placements of existing sections, not fitted joins — the number says how far from a smooth crown each one is.

Capitals · the halves branch outward

The same sections, set at the top of a stem with the far end outward. The halves now diverge instead of meeting: Euler, steep recovery 14.1°, log spiral 49.0°, squircle, 45° arc 11.6°. A capital is the arch rule read backwards — the join is at the bottom, and the curve is free at the top.

Why these six

The sections were not chosen by eye. Each one was placed both ways and measured, and the join decided the use. An arch has to arrive near vertical (≤ 20° off); a capital has to leave the stem outward, between 10° and 90°, so it is a branch and not a fold.

The rejects are the useful part: the log spiral is 49° outward (a capital) but 100° at the crown, so it is not an arch; the cycloid is a good arch (3.9°) but folds back 143° as a capital. A section is a shape, not a role — the placement and the join give it one.
