Integer PH curves: the generator, the grid, and what is actually true

A corrected version of a survey that has been circulating in this project. Where it agrees with the arithmetic it is kept; where it does not, the number below was computed, not remembered. Every figure on this page comes from scripts/generators.py, which works in fractions.Fraction and asserts that each coordinate is integral before it writes anything.

The generator

A planar curve has a Pythagorean hodograph when the speed is a polynomial:

x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2 = sigma(t)^2

Every such curve is written, with complex polynomials w = u + iv and a factor h,

z'(t) = h(t) · ( u(t)^2 - v(t)^2 ,  2 u(t) v(t) )
sigma = h(t) · ( u(t)^2 + v(t)^2 )

and Farouki and Sakkalis's lemma fixes the degree:

n = deg h + 2 · max(deg u, deg v) + 1

That identity is the load-bearing one, and the survey omitted it — which is why its quartics and sextics had no construction. With h = 1 only odd degrees appear; the h factor is the only way to reach an even degree:

deg h max(deg u, deg v) degree
0 1 3
1 1 4
0 2 5
2 1 5
1 2 6

What comes free

With integer generators, and no approximation anywhere:

  • speed is the polynomial h (u² + v²);
  • arc length integrates it exactly, as a polynomial;
  • tangent is 2·atan(v/u), or in integers, Euclid's doubling (u²−v², 2uv) / (u²+v²);
  • offset by a distance d is rational, not refitted;
  • curvature is a rational function;
  • splitting at any t keeps the curve PH and keeps the generators integral.

The integer arithmetic

Control points come from integrating the hodograph, so the cubic divides by 3, the quartic by 4, the quintic by 5, the sextic by 6 — then a conversion to the Bernstein basis multiplies by binomials. Milestones at t = k/m bring the Bernstein weights, which carry m to the degree. The least integer that clears both is the scale.

What that means in practice, for four curves built from small numbers:

curve n generators step scale integer grid
cubic 3 h=(1) u=(2,-2) v=(1,-2) 1/4 24 32 × 32
quartic 4 h=(1,2) u=(1,-1) v=(1,-2) 1/4 1536 1152 × 1280
quintic 5 h=(1) u=(-2,-1,1) v=(-1,-1,2) 1/2 480 1872 × 1744
sextic 6 h=(1,2) u=(-1,1,0) v=(2,-1,0) 1/2 240 880 × 640

Three consequences, and they contradict the folklore.

The milestone scale is mⁿ, and PH never improves it. A plain Bézier whose control points are integers already has integer control points at scale 1, and pays exactly the same mⁿ for its milestones. PH costs a factor of 3 (cubic) or 15 (quintic) on the control points, because the integration denominator has to be cleared. What PH buys is exact arc length, rational offsets and a rational frame — not a smaller lattice.

Integer milestones always exist. Any finite set of rational parameters clears at the lowest common multiple of its denominators. The bean, for example, has quarter-points with denominators 1, 91, 3, 91 and 1, so a scale of 273 puts every one on the grid. The scale is a fact about the arithmetic, not a wall.

What is impossible is the strong claim. No bounded curve holds infinitely many lattice points, so "infinitely many points on the curve can be integers" cannot be true of any curve, PH or not. You get finitely many, at parameters you choose.

None of these are fatal — they are all the same error, which is treating a number that belongs to one set of generators as if it belonged to the degree.

Degree by degree

  • n = 2 does not exist. There are no quadratic PH curves.
  • n = 3 is the Tschirnhausen cubic, up to scale and rotation: one shape only, no inflection, so a C-curve. Loops and cusps are reachable by letting the generators cross.
  • n = 4 needs deg h = 1. It can inflect, which the cubic cannot.
  • n = 5 is the workhorse: C and S shapes, first-order Hermite interpolation with a single segment, and the only low degree that supports C² PH splines. The quintic transition curve — straight line to circular arc with monotone curvature — is the lowest-degree polynomial that does the clothoid's job.
  • n = 6 takes deg h = 1 with quadratic generators. This is where the multiple-cusp shapes live.

Named curves

Two of these are nodal cubics, and only one is the PH one. Every cubic PH curve is a scaled, rotated Tschirnhausen cubic, 27ay² = (a−x)(8a+x)², whose node crosses at 60 degrees. The other, y² = x²(x+1), crosses at 90 — same genus, same degree, reachable from the first only by an affine map, never by a similarity, because an angle is not affine. A cubic PH curve's control polygon is what the top of this page has been building; the node angle is how you tell whether a nodal cubic is that family or merely nearby.

The test that separates these is rationality, and it is decidable: count the singular points, and check that the gradient vanishes at each. A rational curve has an exact Bézier — substitute t = s/(1−s) and clear denominators. A curve with a cusp does not, and sampling is the honest fallback.

The bean is worth spelling out. In polar form it is r = cosθ / (1 − ¼sin²2θ), a ratio of polynomials; with t = tan(θ/2) and t = s/(1−s) it becomes an exact rational Bézier whose homogeneous control values are whole numbers, and it reproduces the curve to 10⁻¹⁶. Its two W = 0 control values are points at infinity, which is why there is no control polygon to lay on the lattice.

The piriform is the fourth exact one. Its equation is a⁴y² = b²x³(2a − x), which at a = b = 1 is y² = x³(2 − x): a rational quartic with a cusp at the origin, a vertex at (2, 0), and the bounds 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, |y| ≤ 3√3/4. Through x = 1 + sin t, y = cos t (1 + sin t), then u = tan(t/2), the homogeneous generator is X = (u+1)²(1+u²), Y = (u+1)²(1−u²), W = (1+u²)² — whole numbers, no clearing needed.

Two wrong forms are worth recording, because both look plausible. The quartic x⁴ − x²y + y² = 0 is a quadratic in y with discriminant −3x⁴, so its only real point is the origin; and x⁴ + x²y − y² = 0 factorises as (y − k₁x²)(y − k₂x²) with k = (1 ± √5)/2 — two parabolas, not a pear. Only the x³ form is the curve.

The catenary

A true catenary is a·cosh(x/a): transcendental, so no scale factor can put it on an integer grid, and the AHPH family keeps the PH property in a hyperbolic basis — exact arc length, rational offsets — but not integers.

This is solved, on its own page. workbench/catenary.html builds the catenary two ways and neither pretends to be a single integer curve:

  • four PH cubics over the curve, exact at both endpoints and both tangents, worst error 0.41 per cent of a;
  • an integer chord walk where the heading snaps to a rung of the 3-4-5 ladder and each chord is the hypotenuse of a real triangle, so every node is a whole number on the lattice.

That is the answer, and it is recorded in state/ACHIEVEMENTS.md. Do not solve it again.

What this session measured

Two results, both negative, both worth keeping.

The discriminant route does not produce a lattice. It is true that a segment's closure quadratic has a rational square discriminant exactly when the fit is exact. But on the full 92-rung pythagorean ladder, for the four catenary chords, 0 of 17,168 non-negative discriminants are squares; and where one is, k carries a denominator in the thousands, so the control points are rational, not integral. Choosing the pair buys exactness, not integers.

The generator route does. You do not fit through prescribed points and hope; you choose integers and the endpoint is whatever it is. That is the difference between scripts/generators.py and a least-squares fit.

Along the way from_pairs in scripts/hodograph.py turned out to rotate by half the tangent angle, so it only closed when a segment started at zero. It is fixed and now agrees with from_hermite to 10⁻¹⁴.
