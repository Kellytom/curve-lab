PH quintics: where the work stands

A senior review of the PH quintic work: the result, what is solid, what is not, and the plan for the next level - finding every integer hit once a curve is chosen.

Live page: workbench/ph-quintic-gallery.html (figures Q1-Q13).

1. The result

The work has a closed-form account of the PH quintic, and it is correct.

Write the speed path in the centred frame, h = t - 1/2:

w(h) = speedMid + slopeMid*h + curvatureMid*h^2
speedMid     = w(1/2)
slopeMid     = w'(1/2)
curvatureMid = w''(1/2)/2

Then, exactly:

arc   = |speedMid|^2 + (|slopeMid|^2 + 2 speedMid.curvatureMid)/12 + |curvatureMid|^2/80
chord = speedMid^2 + (2 speedMid*curvatureMid + slopeMid^2)/12 + curvatureMid^2/80
bend  = arc / |chord|

The weights 1, 1/12, 1/80 are the integrals of 1, h^2, h^4 over [-1/2, 1/2]. The chord is a sum of three complex vectors, so the bend is large exactly when they cancel.

With slopeMid = 0 the three directions are locked to 0, phi, 2phi and the lengths to 1, x/6, x^2/80, where x = |curvatureMid|/|speedMid|. That gives a short dictionary of classic shapes:

shape condition numbers
first and middle terms equal legs length(T1) = length(T2) x = 6
first and third terms equal legs length(T1) = length(T3) x = 4*sqrt(5)
middle and third terms equal legs length(T2) = length(T3) x = 40/3
right angle, first to middle phi = 90 deg any x
right angle, first to third phi = 45 deg any x
the triangle closes chord = 0 x = 4*sqrt(5), phi = 138.19 deg

The closure is exact: chord = speedMid^2 (1 + ratio/6 + ratio^2/80) with ratio = curvatureMid/speedMid, which vanishes at ratio = -20/3 +/- (8*sqrt(5)/3) i, i.e. |ratio| = 4*sqrt(5) ~ 8.94 at curvatureAngle = 138.19 deg.

The formula also inverts. Given the three chord terms:

speedMid     = sqrt(T1)
curvatureMid = sqrt(80*T3)
slopeMid     = sqrt(12*T2 - 2 speedMid*curvatureMid)

Each square root has two signs, so one diagram gives up to eight curves - mirrors and rotations of one shape. This is the design tool: draw the diagram, solve for the recipe.

2. What is solid

  • Exactness is the house rule. Recipes are stored as exact fractions, never as decimals, and the pitch is the lcm of the integrated coefficients' denominators. A decimal like 1.5 costs pitch; the JSON records the cost.
  • Pages are generated. Nothing on the gallery is hand-drawn; the board and the sheets are built from the case tables.
  • Naming is now written down (AGENTS.md): camelCase in JavaScript, kebab ids prefixed by figure, snake_case in Python, and every maths symbol gets a searchable name.
  • The data files are tested. 16 presets, 10 saved curves, and the numbers are checked against the generator.
  • The maths has been verified twice, independently, in Python and in JavaScript, on every preset.

3. Defects, in priority order

3.1 The exact formula lives only in JavaScript

The page states that the three lines reproduce every preset to within 1e-4, but scripts/ph_quintic_presets.py still computes bend_ratio by sampling 800 points. The JSON is therefore approximate while advertising exactness, and the formula cannot be reused by a test, a script, or the integer-hit work.

Fix: one exact_bend(u, v) in the library, used by the presets, the saved-curve cleaner and the page generator.

3.2 Four JavaScript implementations of the same maths

workbench/ph-quintic-gallery.html carries six <script> blocks, 22 copies of evalQuadratic and six pitch implementations. They have already drifted: Q8 has 12 preset buttons, the preset JSON has 16 presets.

Fix: one workbench/js/ph-panels.js with metrics, drawCurve, buildPanel, chordTerms; each figure becomes a config object.

3.3 The generator is 1647 lines and its JavaScript is untested

Prose, Python and JavaScript live in one string, so a replace can break a panel. It has: Q7 lost its sliders once, and mTop leaked into Q7 once. Neither was caught by a test.

Fix: split data from template, and add one headless check that loads the page and asserts the six readouts (1.15, 5.58, 2.00, 1.91, 2.04, 2.00). That is twenty lines and it would have caught both breaks.

3.4 Floats leak into exact arithmetic

Fraction(0.2) is the exact binary fraction. It produced a pitch of 1.6e33, then 9.3e107 in saved curves. Both were fixed by rounding, in two different places.

Fix: one to_fraction(value, max_decimals=6) that refuses silent binary noise, and a test that Fraction(float) appears nowhere else.

3.5 The saved-curve loop is not closed

Saved curves live in localStorage; the operator must paste JSON back, and then a script cleans it. A small merge step would close the loop: read the clipboard or a dropped file, clean it, append to the preset JSON.

3.6 The page is 664 KB

Inline SVG for 48 thumbnails. Fine locally, heavy for a deploy. Not urgent.

4. The next level: integer hits

Given a chosen curve r(t) = x(t) + i y(t) with rational coefficients, find every lattice point it passes through. Three methods, and they are not equivalent.

4.1 The pitch stops - fast, not complete

Sample at t = m/pitch. Exact at those stops, O(pitch), and they are the canonical grid. But they miss hits: for x = t, y = t^2 the pitch is 1 and the integer points are at every integer t; the stops see only t = 0, 1.

4.2 The exact method - complete

For each lattice point (X, Y) in the bounding box, compute gcd(x(t) - X, y(t) - Y) over the rationals. A non-constant gcd means the point is on the curve, and its roots give the parameters - rational or algebraic. The box is a few hundred wide and the polynomials are degree 5, so this is cheap and complete.

4.3 The midpoint method - a different question

Sample at cell centres, t = (2m+1)/(2*pitch), and measure the distance to the nearest lattice point. That is a near-miss report: how close the curve comes to the grid between stops. It is not a hit list.

4.4 The API

hits_at_stops(u, v)        # O(pitch), exact, the canonical grid
hits_exact(u, v, box)      # bounding box + polynomial gcd, complete
near_misses(u, v, box)     # midpoint: distance to the nearest lattice point

Tests: the pick must give (15, 4) at pitch 10; a curve with a hit off the stops must find it; a curve with none must return none.

6. What the literature confirms

Checked against the PH-curve literature (August 2026). Four things match, and one naming point is worth fixing.

  • Odd degree is a theorem, not an accident. Farouki and Sakkalis, Pythagorean hodographs, IBM J. Res. Dev. 34 (1990) 736-752: the degree is n = lambda + 2p + 1, and "every primitive PH curve is thus of odd degree". So degree 6 cannot be a polynomial PH curve; degree 7 is a cubic pre-image. That is the parity the sandbox states.
  • Eight knobs is the right count. A PH curve of degree n has at most n + 3 degrees of freedom; the primitive degree-7 curve is generated by a cubic pre-image, four complex numbers - eight real coefficients. Exactly the eight sliders in panel B, and no more.
  • Degree 7 is the curvature-continuous workhorse. Juttler, Hermite interpolation by Pythagorean hodograph curves of degree seven, Math. Comp. 70 (2001) 1089-1111: G2 Hermite data is matched with up to eight solutions from two quartics, and the cubic pre-image is the choice that allows the maximum two inflections. So the septic is not an exotic step; it is the standard next rung.
  • Closure has a name. A closed PH curve needs the control-polygon legs to sum to zero, plus an arc-length constraint to avoid degenerating to a point (see Construction of planar quintic PH curves by control-polygon constraints, CAGD 2023, remark 2). Our sum of T_m = 0 is that condition in the power basis, and the arc-length constraint is the one we have not yet imposed.

The naming point: our T_m are the power-basis coefficients of r(t), while the literature's "legs" L_i are the Bernstein differences. Both sum to the same chord, so the closure test is identical, but a reader coming from the PH papers will expect legs.

What this does not change: the exact bend formula, the inverse, the hit methods and the parity statement all stand. What it adds: the septic has a standard literature to lean on for the G2 interpolation step, and the closed-curve construction has a known extra condition (arc length) that we can adopt when the closed loop matters.
