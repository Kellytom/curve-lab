2. Already winding: the rule

A spiral that enters already curved can wind many turns inside the same circle. The radial gap between successive windings is about r / (2N) for N turns at final radius r. Setting that equal to the stroke width w gives N = r / (2w). The outer radius needed is about r √N, so the box grows as 2r √N.

The “it looks like a circle” point and the “the windings touch” point are the same event: the radius changes by 1/(2N) per turn, which is 5% at N=10 and 2% at N=25, by which time the gap is smaller than any drawn line.

4. Is there a maximum and minimum circle?

Nothing in the clothoid has a size: scale it and every length scales with it. On a bounded page the limits are the page and the stroke width.

  • Largest full circle: half the page, r = L/2. At L = 100 that is r = 50, a 100-wide circle touching the edges. Larger radii still appear as arcs, never as a closed circle in the box.
  • Smallest circle with a stroke: r = 2w, where w is the stroke width. At r = 2w the inner hole is exactly one stroke wide; any smaller and the ribbon has no hole and reads as a disc. With one-point drafting points there is also a floor at r = 0.5, but the stroke is almost always the binding limit.
  • The turn budget: a winding spiral of N turns at radius r needs a box about 2r√N, and its gap is r/(2N). Both must hold, so N ≤ min(r/(2w), (L/2r)²). The two limits meet at r* = (wL²/2)^(1/3), which is the radius that buys the most turns without merging.

For a 100 grid and a 1-point line: the smallest circle is r = 2 (4 across), the largest is r = 50 (100 across), and the best compromise is r ≈ 17 with about 8.5 turns. A 5-unit ribbon moves the best radius to ≈ 29 and cuts the turns to about 3. That is the real trade: the wider the stroke, the larger the circle must be, and the fewer turns it can carry.

7. Outward from the circle — the special case

The special case is a spiral that becomes a circle. At the join the clothoid's curvature is 1/r, exactly the circle's, so the tangent and the curvature match: G2, no kink. Start on the circle and unwind outward; the curve ends as it approaches R = 49. The gap grows like r³, so the join is the tightest point. Set the gap there to the clearance c and the rest is legal by construction.

At r = 5 and c = 1 pt, the spiral makes about 2.5 turns to R = 49, and the gap at the edge is about 941 pt: the outer turns are very wide. For a fixed clearance, the radius that buys the most turns is R/√3 ≈ 28.3, at about 9.4 turns. That is the radius to solve for when the turn count is the goal; r = 5 is the radius to solve for when the circle is the goal.

What the napkin got right, and what it missed

The 80 box and a 30-wide final circle are right: the computed box is 80.1 × 73.4, and the ink ends at (64.7, 44.9). The centre is about (51, 51), not 65 — 65 is where the ink stops, not the middle of the circle. The missed part is the count: a clothoid from rest spends most of its length almost straight, so only about one turn is left in the box. To get many turns you must start already curving (an entry radius), which is the same object trimmed.

With a finite entry radius R the turn count is set by (R/r)². For R = 41 and r = 15 that is 7.5 turns — exactly the collision count for a 1-point line. That is the real answer: about seven and a half turns, and the last gap is one unit.
